# Research Brief: Design Menu Cards and Decision Tables

## FTC YES Course Design

## Course Design Feature

The FTC YES course uses design menu cards and decision tables to help students make engineering choices. These menus present options, trade-offs, and evidence prompts before teams build, code, test, or request scarce materials.

Examples include:

- chassis design menu,
- wheel layout menu,
- motor placement menu,
- D-pad vs. joystick control menu,
- servo trigger menu,
- shooter design menu,
- materials request menu,
- team norms menu,
- and academic challenge menu.

## Problem This Process Addresses

Beginning robotics students often do not know what decisions they are making. They may treat the first idea as the only idea, copy a mentor's suggestion without understanding it, or build before naming the constraints.

Design menu cards are meant to make invisible decisions visible.

## Description of the Problem

A robotics team may think it is simply "building a chassis," but it is actually making many connected decisions:

- What frame system should we use?
- What shape should the frame be?
- Should the robot be wide, narrow, open, or compact?
- Where should the drive motors go?
- Where should the battery and electronics go?
- Which wheels are powered?
- How much speed do we want?
- What are we giving up if we choose this option?

Without scaffolding, students may not recognize these as design decisions. More confident students may push a preferred idea, while less experienced students may not know enough to participate. This can lead to weaker designs, less shared ownership, and fewer opportunities for students to practice engineering reasoning.

## Research Summary

Engineering design is fundamentally about managing trade-offs under constraints. Purzer et al. (2015) found that high school students made stronger science connections during engineering design when they engaged in trade-off analysis, experimentation, data gathering, and reflection. The authors concluded that design projects should emphasize trade-off analysis and include time and resources to support trade-off decisions.

Recent design research also shows that real engineering trade-offs are shaped by context. Nickel et al. (2024) found that designers' trade-off decisions are influenced by users, resources, regulations, team norms, prior decisions, and incomplete information. This matters for robotics because teams are not making purely technical choices. Their decisions are shaped by time, available parts, student skill, borrowed materials, robot rules, safety, strategy, and repair access.

Research on collaborative engineering projects also warns that access to meaningful design work can be shaped by social hierarchy. Henderson (2023) found that students' prior experience, confidence, and perceived skills influenced how roles and learning opportunities were negotiated in engineering teams. Similarly, Quan et al. (2015) argue that group composition and emergent roles shape students' access to design and coding experiences in K-12 design teams.

Design menu cards respond to these findings by giving all students shared language for the decision, not just the students who already have technical vocabulary.

## Why This Course Design Is a Good Response

Menu cards support engineering learning because they:

- name the decision area,
- show multiple possible choices,
- explain advantages and trade-offs,
- give students vocabulary,
- help less experienced students enter the conversation,
- slow down premature building,
- and connect decisions to portfolio evidence.

The decision table turns the menu into documentation:

## Expected Student Gains

Students should improve in:

- recognizing design choices,
- naming constraints,
- comparing alternatives,
- explaining trade-offs,
- using evidence before and after decisions,
- connecting robot design to game strategy,
- participating in technical discussions,
- and documenting engineering reasoning.

Design menu cards should also reduce overdependence on the teacher. Instead of asking, "What should we do?" students can ask, "Which option best fits our goal, and what trade-off are we accepting?"

## Examples of Desired Outcomes

A team using the chassis menu might write:

| Decision Area | Our Choice | Why We Chose It | What We Gave Up | Evidence We Will Watch |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Frame shape | U-shaped chassis | Leaves space for balls to enter from the front | May be less rigid without support | Does frame twist during turning? |
| Wheel layout | 2 drive wheels + 2 omni wheels | Easier turning for new drivers | Less pushing traction | Turning radius and driver control |
| Battery placement | Low and near center | Helps balance and reduces tipping | Slightly harder to remove | Battery change time and stability |

A team using the control-code menu might write:

| Decision Area | Our Choice | Why We Chose It | What We Gave Up | Evidence We Will Watch |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Driver control | Joystick with dead zone | Smoother driving and less drift | More complicated code than D-pad | Driver confidence and course time |

## Potential Obstacles

Likely challenges include:

- students treating menus as worksheets instead of thinking tools,
- teams trying to fill every box before building,
- dominant students choosing quickly and asking others to copy,
- students assuming the "Could Do" option is automatically better,
- teams failing to revisit decisions after testing,
- and teachers overloading students with too many options at once.

## Implementation Strategies

Use menu cards as short, practical scaffolds:

1. Introduce only the menu needed for the current task.
2. Pair the menu with a concrete sketch, code, or test deliverable.
3. Require teams to identify at least two options before choosing.
4. Ask teams to name one trade-off for each major decision.
5. Require one piece of evidence the team will watch during testing.
6. Revisit the table after testing and ask: Did the evidence support our choice?

Useful teacher prompts:

- "What are your options?"
- "What does this choice help?"
- "What does this choice make harder?"
- "What evidence would tell you this was a good choice?"
- "Who has not spoken yet?"
- "Is this good enough to test?"

Design menu cards connect to:

- project-based learning,

## Notes on Evidence Quality

The research base for this brief includes high school engineering design research, professional design research, and collaborative engineering education research. There is less direct research on "menu cards" as a named robotics strategy, so this brief treats menu cards as a practical scaffold for research-supported practices: trade-off analysis, multiple-option comparison, role access, reflection, and evidence-based design decisions.
