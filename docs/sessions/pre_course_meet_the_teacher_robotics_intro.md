# Meet the Teacher Session: Robotics Course Introduction

## Suggested File Location

`docs/sessions/pre_course_meet_the_teacher_robotics_intro.md`

## Session Context

This is a short pre-course “meet the teacher” session for students before the official 20-session robotics course begins.

This session should **not** replace Day 1 / Session 1. Some students may miss this meeting, so nothing in this session should be required for students to succeed later. Treat this as a course trailer: build curiosity, explain the purpose of the course, introduce the culture, and invite students to begin thinking about their interests and strengths.

## Session Length

Approximately 25 minutes

## Co-Teachers

- Mr. Fitzgerald
- Mrs. Probric

## Big Goal

Students leave with an honest, exciting picture of the course:

> This course is about learning engineering by doing engineering. It will be hands-on, team-based, creative, uncertain, sometimes chaotic, and focused on solving real problems together.

## Learning Targets

By the end of this short session, students will be able to:

- Describe the general purpose of the robotics course.
- Explain that robotics connects math, science, coding, design, teamwork, and communication.
- Understand that the course will involve challenge by choice, shared expertise, and no upper limits.
- Recognize that the course may feel messy or chaotic because real engineering is uncertain.
- Identify at least one robotics role, interest area, or team strength they may want to explore.

## Materials

- Projector or screen
- FIRST / FTC impact video
- Short FTC robot, game, or match video
- Google Form exit survey
- Optional: robot parts, drivetrain, controller, or team artifacts for students to see
- Optional: QR code or short link to the Google Form

## Teacher Opening Script

Use or adapt the following:

> Welcome. This course is about robotics, but it is also about teamwork, problem-solving, design, failure, revision, and presentation.
>
> You do not need to already know how to build or code robots. You do need to be willing to try things, work with people, be safe, and learn from problems.
>
> This is not the official first lesson of the course. Some students may not be here today, so we will officially start together later. Today is a preview of what this course is about and why Mrs. Probric and I think it matters.

## Why We Chose Robotics

Use or adapt the following:

> I want to tell you why I chose this course and why I care about it.
>
> I have coached FIRST robotics for more than 15 years, from elementary school through high school. I am certified in both math and science, and outside of teaching I also work as a developer. With FIRST teams, I have helped students launch three apps on the App Store and Google Play.
>
> Mrs. Probric and I are both math teachers, and we are both coders. That matters because we see robotics as more than building a machine. We see it as a place where math, science, computer science, engineering, design, writing, teamwork, and presentation all come together.
>
> In a normal class, it is easy to think the goal is to get the right answer. In robotics, the goal is different. The robot either works or it does not, and when it does not, the team has to figure out why.
>
> That process can be frustrating, but it is also powerful. Students learn to build, code, test, communicate, lead, document, and present. They also learn that they can contribute even if they are not already “robotics people.”
>
> This is the part of teaching where I feel most alive. It is my favorite thing to do with students because I get to watch students discover that they can build things, solve problems, lead teams, explain ideas, and create something that did not exist before.

## FIRST / FTC Message

Use or adapt the following:

> FIRST is powerful because it puts students in a real team situation where they work together to solve a real problem.
>
> Adults guide, coach, support, and keep students safe, but the students are supposed to own the problem. The students build the robot. The students write the code. The students test, revise, present, and improve.
>
> FIRST sometimes says it is “the only sport where everyone can go pro.” I like that idea because the skills you build in robotics do not only matter for robotics. They matter in engineering, business, medicine, science, design, construction, computer science, education, and almost every field where people solve problems.
>
> But I do not want to oversell this class. This course will not magically turn everyone into an engineer. Some days will be frustrating. Parts will not fit. Code will not work. Teams will disagree. That is part of the experience.
>
> What I do believe is this: if you take this seriously, you will leave this course better at solving problems with other people. That is one of the most valuable skills you can build.

## Video 1: FIRST / FTC Impact Video

Before playing the video, say:

> As you watch, look for evidence that students are doing more than building a robot. Look for teamwork, confidence, leadership, problem-solving, communication, and persistence.

After the video, ask:

- What did you notice?
- What looked exciting?
- What looked challenging?
- What did students seem to be learning besides robotics?
- What kinds of people or roles seemed useful on a robotics team?

## Video 2: FTC Robot / Game Clip

Before playing the video, say:

> The first video showed the bigger purpose. This next clip shows the kind of challenge teams actually work on. As you watch, look for robot systems and team decisions.

After the video, ask:

- What systems did you notice on the robot?
- What do you think had to be designed?
- What do you think had to be coded?
- What do you think teams had to test and improve?
- What could go wrong with a robot like this?

## Course Culture

### Challenge by Choice

Use or adapt:

> One phrase I want you to hear early is “challenge by choice.”
>
> That means we are going to invite you to stretch. We are going to ask you to try things that may feel new or uncomfortable. But we are not here to embarrass you, label you, or decide on day one what you are capable of.
>
> You will have choices. You can grow into roles. You can start small and build confidence.

### No Upper Limits

Use or adapt:

> Another idea is “no upper limits.”
>
> We are not going to put a ceiling on what you can learn. Some students may start by learning how to safely use tools. Some may start by learning basic robot code. Some may go much farther: designing mechanisms, programming sensors, improving strategy, documenting the engineering process, or leading a team.
>
> If you want to keep going deeper, we will try to support that.

### Coopertition and Shared Expertise

Use or adapt:

> FIRST also uses an idea called Coopertition. That means competition and cooperation can exist at the same time.
>
> We want to do well, but we also want other people to get better. In this class, expertise should be shared. If you figure something out, part of your job is to help someone else understand it. If another team solves a problem before you do, we can learn from them.
>
> That is not cheating. That is engineering culture.

### Chaos and No Guaranteed Success

Use or adapt:

> I also want to be honest about something important.
>
> This course will sometimes feel chaotic. Not because we do not have a plan, but because real engineering is messy. When you are solving a real problem, there is no answer key. The first design usually does not work. Code breaks. Parts do not fit. Teams disagree. A test that should work might fail for reasons nobody expected.
>
> That is not a flaw in the course. That is the course.
>
> There is no guarantee that every robot will work perfectly. There is no guarantee that every idea will succeed. What we can guarantee is that you will have the chance to design, build, test, revise, explain, and improve.
>
> Success in this class is not just “Did the robot work?” Success is also: Did your team learn something? Did you solve a problem you could not solve before? Did you help someone else? Did you document your thinking? Did you keep going when the first version failed?
>
> We are not promising a smooth experience. We are promising a real one.

## Connection to Engineering Bios

Today should seed the engineering bio assignment, not replace it.

Use or adapt:

> Later in the course, you will create a short engineering bio. This is not about proving that you already know robotics. It is about helping your team understand who you are, what you are curious about, what strengths you bring, and what you want to learn.
>
> You do not need to bring a finished bio next class. We will work on it together. Today, I just want you to start thinking about what kind of teammate you want to be.

## Google Form Exit Survey

### Suggested Title

Robotics Course Interest Form

### Suggested Description

This is not a test and there are no wrong answers. We are using this to learn about your interests, strengths, and goals so we can build strong teams and support your growth in robotics.

### Suggested Questions

#### 1. Name

Short answer.

> What is your name?

#### 2. Interest Areas

Checkboxes.

> Which parts of robotics might you be interested in? Check any that apply.

Choices:

- Building and mechanical design
- Programming/coding
- Driving the robot
- Game strategy
- Math/physics/science connections
- Computer-aided design or sketching
- Wiring/electronics
- Documenting the engineering process
- Presenting/explaining the robot
- Team leadership/organization
- I am not sure yet
- Other

#### 3. Current Feeling

Multiple choice.

> Which statement feels most true right now?

Choices:

- I am excited and ready to jump in.
- I am interested but a little nervous.
- I am not sure what to expect yet.
- I do not think robotics is my thing yet, but I am open to trying.

#### 4. Excitement

Short answer.

> What is one thing about this course that seems exciting?

#### 5. Challenge

Short answer.

> What is one thing about this course that might feel challenging?

#### 6. Team Strength

Short answer.

> What is one strength you could bring to a team?

#### 7. Learning Goal

Short answer.

> What is one thing you might want to learn or get better at in this course?

#### 8. Productive Struggle

Multiple choice.

> This course may sometimes feel messy or frustrating because teams will be solving real problems without an answer key. How do you usually respond when something does not work the first time?

Choices:

- I usually want to try again right away.
- I usually need a minute, but then I can try again.
- I usually get frustrated and need help getting restarted.
- I am not sure yet.

#### 9. Support

Optional short answer.

> What helps you do your best work when you are learning something new or working with a team?

#### 10. Anything Else

Optional short answer.

> Is there anything you want Mrs. Probric or Mr. Fitzgerald to know before the course begins?

## What Not to Do Today

Avoid turning this into the official first class.

Do not:

- Require students to complete a formal bio.
- Present the full 20-session plan in detail.
- Make today’s survey the official baseline for the impact study.
- Grade participation.
- Assign required homework that absent students will miss.
- Overpromise that every robot will work or that the course will be smooth.

## Follow-Up After Session

After the session:

1. Review Google Form responses.
2. Look for students who are excited, nervous, uncertain, or already experienced.
3. Use responses to inform Day 1 grouping and discussion.
4. Save the official baseline impact survey for Day 1.
5. Keep today’s form as an interest snapshot, not the formal pre-course measure.
