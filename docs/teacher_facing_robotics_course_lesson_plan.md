# Robotics Course Teacher Lesson Plan

## 1. Course Purpose

This course uses robotics as an entry point into advanced STEM learning. Students work in teams to design, build, program, test, and present a robot while connecting their work to engineering, computer science, mathematics, physics, data analysis, careers, and their own strengths. The course emphasizes curiosity, collaboration, risk-taking, iteration, and student-led problem solving.

This is a 10-day, 20-session modified FIRST Tech Challenge course. It is not intended to recreate a full FTC season in two weeks. Instead, it gives students a compact, authentic experience with FTC-style teamwork, robot design, Java programming, testing, troubleshooting, documentation, presentation, and shared-material stewardship. Students should leave the course more confident trying unfamiliar STEM work and better able to see how robotics connects to AP Computer Science A, AP Physics, AP Calculus, AP Statistics, engineering, design, programming, fabrication, data analysis, and future robotics-team participation.

The course culture is low-floor and high-ceiling. Every student should participate in building, testing, coding, documenting, and presenting in some way. Challenge by Choice pathways give students ways to go deeper without locking them into permanent specialist roles.

## 2. Course Design Principles

- Student-led problem solving: students discuss, decide, test, and revise instead of only following teacher directions.
- Modified FIRST Tech Challenge structure: teams use FTC-style parts, vocabulary, programming, documentation, inspection, and presentation habits.
- Teams of 4-5: teams should be small enough for everyone to have real work.
- Engineering portfolio documentation: teams collect evidence of decisions, sketches, build progress, code, tests, revisions, and reflection.
- Robot demonstration: each team should demonstrate what the robot can do and be honest about its limitations.
- Must / Should / Could framework: sessions should identify a minimum useful task, a stronger target, and an optional extension.
- Super Challenges: optional depth pathways in design/fabrication, programming, and data/physics.
- Risk-taking and iteration: students should expect first attempts to fail, then improve from evidence.
- Read-discuss-design-build-test-document-reflect cycle: not every session includes every part, but the course rhythm should stay visible.
- Connections to STEM pathways: teachers should explicitly connect robot work to FTC, AP courses, engineering, computer science, data science, design, manufacturing, aviation/motorsports inspection, and technical communication.

## 3. Typical Session Routine

Most sessions use this general rhythm:

1. State the purpose or learning goal.
2. Use a short background reading, handout, or technical preparation.
3. Run a student-led discussion or team decision prompt.
4. Have teams make or revise a design, strategy, build, programming, or testing choice.
5. Provide work time for fabrication, assembly, programming, testing, data analysis, documentation, or presentation.
6. Check deliverables.
7. Update the engineering portfolio.
8. Close with a short reflection or share-out.

Not every session includes every component. Some sessions are mostly assembly, some are mostly programming, some are mostly testing, and some are mostly documentation or presentation. The teacher's job is to keep the work purposeful and evidence-based without overloading students with paperwork.

## 4. Assessment / Products

Each team should produce:

- A working or partially working FTC-style robot.
- A robot demonstration.
- An engineering portfolio.
- A final team presentation or explanation.
- Evidence of team decisions, testing, revision, and reflection.
- Optional Super Challenge evidence if students choose deeper pathways.

The engineering portfolio should include:

- Student-led discussion records.
- Team name, team norms, and personal goals.
- Design sketches and annotated diagrams.
- Decision tables and trade-off notes.
- Build photos with captions.
- Code notes, code screenshots, OpMode names, configuration names, or code files.
- Testing results, failed test notes, successful test notes, measurements, driver feedback, and next-step decisions.
- Problems encountered and revisions made.
- Evidence from optional Challenge by Choice pathways.
- Final presentation planning and final reflection.

Assessment should value contribution, evidence, iteration, safety, communication, and growth. A robot that worked imperfectly but was tested honestly and explained clearly can show strong engineering learning.

## 5. Deliverables System

Students should produce evidence during each session. Not every session requires every type of evidence, but each session should have clearly identified deliverables.

Common deliverables include:

- SLD document: a student-led discussion record showing the team's decision-making process.
- Design drawing: sketch, diagram, CAD screenshot, labeled mechanism drawing, or annotated plan.
- Build photos: photos of the chassis, mechanism, prototype, wiring, sensor placement, or test setup.
- Code: any OpMode, function, pseudocode, commented code block, configuration notes, or code revision.
- Testing results: data table, observations, failed test notes, successful test notes, measurements, driver feedback, or next-step decisions.
- Engineering portfolio entry: a short written entry explaining what the team did, what decision they made, what evidence they used, and what they need to do next.

The SLD document captures the team's decision-making process. The engineering portfolio is the final organized record of the team's work. Students may create separate SLD notes, sketches, photos, code, and test results during the session, but the important evidence should eventually be summarized or linked in the engineering portfolio.

Use the templates in `02_student_materials/engineering_notebook/student_engineering_notebook_master.md` as the working portfolio structure. Useful recurring templates include the daily engineering log, design decision record, test data table, code change log, failure/fix record, photo evidence checklist, final reflection, and presentation planning pages.

## 7. Detailed Session Plans

### Day 1, Session 1: FIRST, FTC, Safety, Trust, and Personal Goals

Purpose: Introduce the course as a shared robotics experience rooted in safety, borrowed-equipment stewardship, and personal growth.

Learning Target: Students can explain how this FTC-style course works, how to use shared equipment responsibly, and one way they want to grow.

Success Criteria:

- Students name at least one safety expectation.
- Students describe why shared equipment requires trust.
- Students choose a personal goal area.

Teacher Preparation:

- Prepare orientation files, safety readings, borrowed-equipment expectations, individual goal menu, SLD prompt, and optional student bio.
- Prepare a visible storage/cleanup routine for shared parts.
- Decide how first reflections will be collected.

Student Background Reading / Preparation:

- `02_student_materials/readings/day1_session1_what_is_first.md`
- `02_student_materials/readings/day1_session1_what_is_first_tech_challenge.md`
- `02_student_materials/readings/day1_session1_robotics_safety_norms.md`
- `02_student_materials/readings/day1_session1_borrowed_equipment_expectations.md`
- `02_student_materials/decision_menus/day1_session1_individual_goal_menu.md`
- `02_student_materials/sld_prompts/day1_session1_sld_shared_materials_student.md`

Student-Led Discussion / Decision:

- Prompt: What do we owe to the teams, mentors, and community members who made this robotics equipment available to us?

Work Time Options:

- Documentation: choose personal goal and safety/community commitment.
- Discussion: identify shared-material norms.
- Optional demonstration: observe a robot safely, focusing on systems and caution.

Must / Should / Could:

- Must: Choose one personal goal and record one safety/community commitment.
- Should: Connect the goal to a portfolio evidence type.
- Could: Complete a student bio or identify a Could-level challenge area.

Deliverables:

- SLD note.
- Personal goal choice.
- Safety/community commitment.
- Optional student bio.

Engineering Portfolio Evidence:

- Goal area, why it matters, expected evidence of growth, and one SLD insight.

Reflection / Share-Out:

- What is one safety norm the class must protect?
- What is one way you want to grow during the course?

Teacher Notes:

- Watch for students who need a low-floor entry point.
- Model cleanup and honest reporting immediately.
- Avoid making winning the frame; emphasize building, learning, and stewardship.

### Day 1, Session 2: Collaborative Engineering Teams

Purpose: Establish a course culture where students participate fully and help each other enter unfamiliar work.

Learning Target: Students can describe how they might contribute to an engineering team and how teammates can make room for different strengths.

Success Criteria:

- Students identify more than one type of useful team contribution.
- Students distinguish helping from taking over.
- Students name one behavior that invites participation.

Teacher Preparation:

- Prepare `day1_session2_collaborative_engineering_teams.md`.
- Prepare student bio or contribution notes if using them for team formation.

Student Background Reading / Preparation:

- `02_student_materials/readings/day1_session2_collaborative_engineering_teams.md`
- `02_student_materials/engineering_notebook/day1_session1_student_bio_template.md`

Student-Led Discussion / Decision:

- Prompt: How should an engineering team make room for different kinds of contributors?

Work Time Options:

- Discussion: strengths, quiet voices, and helping without taking over.
- Documentation: individual contribution note.
- Team formation: assign teams of 4-5 if not already assigned.

Must / Should / Could:

- Must: Record one contribution the student can make.
- Should: Identify one kind of work the student wants to try.
- Could: Name a way to help a teammate learn without taking over.

Deliverables:

- Teamwork reflection or bio-based contribution note.
- Optional team formation notes.

Engineering Portfolio Evidence:

- Individual contribution, team behavior to practice, and participation goal.

Reflection / Share-Out:

- What is one kind of work you want to try during the course?
- What does helpful teamwork sound like?

Teacher Notes:

- Use this session to notice leadership patterns.
- Structure entry points for quieter or less experienced students.
- Reinforce that students are not locked into fixed roles.

### Day 2, Session 3: Team Identity and Team Norms

Purpose: Give teams a first low-risk decision-making experience before major robot choices.

Learning Target: Students can help choose a team name and team norms using reasons and trade-offs.

Success Criteria:

- Each team chooses a name.
- Each team chooses norms and explains why they matter.
- Teams record trade-offs and repair agreements.

Teacher Preparation:

- Prepare team assignments, name menu, norms menu, and portfolio recording space.

Student Background Reading / Preparation:

- `02_student_materials/readings/day2_session3_why_team_names_matter.md`
- `02_student_materials/decision_menus/day2_session3_team_name_menu.md`
- `02_student_materials/decision_menus/day2_session3_team_norms_menu.md`

Student-Led Discussion / Decision:

- Prompt: What should our team name and norms say about the team we want to become?

Work Time Options:

- Design: team identity and possible logo/motto.
- Documentation: name and norms decision tables.
- Team process: create a repair agreement for when norms are broken.

Must / Should / Could:

- Must: Choose a team name and at least four norms.
- Should: Explain the trade-off for each norm and create a repair agreement.
- Could: Connect the norms to final presentation or award/recognition goals.

Deliverables:

- SLD document.
- Team name decision table.
- Team norms table.
- Team agreement.

Engineering Portfolio Evidence:

- Final name, selected norms, why they were chosen, and how the team will know they are working.

Reflection / Share-Out:

- Which norm will be hardest to keep when the robot does not work?

Teacher Notes:

- Use decision menus to slow the conversation enough for all students to contribute.
- Watch for joke names that will not support public presentation.

### Day 2, Session 4: First Programming Session

Purpose: Introduce how FTC software connects driver input, Java code, configuration names, and physical hardware.

Learning Target: Students can explain how a simple TeleOp program connects the Driver Station, OpMode, hardware map, and robot hardware.

Success Criteria:

- Students identify the OpMode name.
- Students identify hardware names in code.
- Students explain that configuration names must match quoted names in Java.
- Students record one predicted or observed robot behavior.

Teacher Preparation:

- Prepare Robot Controller/Driver Station devices, OnBot Java access, example TeleOp code, hardware configuration examples, batteries, and safe test blocks.

Student Background Reading / Preparation:

- `02_student_materials/readings/day2_session4_programming_background.md`
- `02_student_materials/readings/day2_session4_reading_basic_teleop.md`

Student-Led Discussion / Decision:

- Prompt: Why must the names in the configuration file match the names in the program exactly?

Work Time Options:

- Programming: read and annotate a basic TeleOp.
- Testing: run only if hardware is ready and safely lifted.
- Documentation: map hardware names, motor ports, variables, and controls.

Must / Should / Could:

- Must: Identify OpMode, hardware names, variables, and gamepad inputs in a basic TeleOp.
- Should: Make one small documented code or configuration change.
- Could: Add telemetry or comments that make the code easier for a teammate to understand.

Deliverables:

- Code notes or screenshot.
- Configuration-name table.
- Test result if hardware is ready.
- Engineering portfolio entry.

Engineering Portfolio Evidence:

- OpMode name, configured hardware names, motor ports, gamepad controls, one code change or prediction, and result.

Reflection / Share-Out:

- What part of the code/configuration chain must be correct for safe movement?

Teacher Notes:

- Keep first code changes small.
- Prioritize safety and conceptual understanding over driving speed.

### Day 3, Session 5: Chassis Design Planning and Build Start

Purpose: Move from planning to physical chassis assembly while protecting documented design choices.

Learning Target: Students can start building a chassis that matches team decisions about size, shape, material, wheels, motors, and electronics space.

Success Criteria:

- Teams have a labeled chassis layout.
- Teams can explain one trade-off.
- Teams begin assembly safely and document first build evidence.

Teacher Preparation:

- Prepare tools, safety glasses, frame parts, brackets, fasteners, wheels, motors, batteries for layout checks, labels, and team storage.

Student Background Reading / Preparation:

- `02_student_materials/readings/day3_session5_chassis_physics_space_background.md`
- `02_student_materials/readings/principles_of_robot_assembly_day3.md`
- Day 2 chassis shape, material, wheel, motor placement, and electronics/battery placement menus.

Student-Led Discussion / Decision:

- Prompt: Which design choice should we protect when the build gets difficult or slow?

Work Time Options:

- Design: final top-view chassis sketch.
- Assembly: frame, brackets, fasteners, wheel/motor fit checks.
- Documentation: build photos and first issue note.

Must / Should / Could:

- Must: Create or confirm a labeled chassis sketch and start a safe frame assembly.
- Should: Photograph the starting frame and record one trade-off.
- Could: Compare two bracket, motor, or electronics layouts using repair access and future mechanism space.

Deliverables:

- SLD decision note.
- Design drawing.
- Build photos.
- Engineering portfolio entry.

Engineering Portfolio Evidence:

- Chassis decision, sketch, parts used, first issue noticed, and next step.

Reflection / Share-Out:

- What design choice became harder once the team started building?

### Day 3, Session 6: Chassis Readiness, Controller Mapping, and Drive Tuning

Purpose: Use chassis readiness as the entry condition for safe first drive, then compare D-pad and joystick controls, collect driving evidence, and make a first controller mapping and drive power/speed decision.

Success Criteria:

- Motors and wheels are secure.
- Wires and electronics have planned space.
- Teams identify readiness issues before turning on the robot.
- Teams compare D-pad and joystick driving with controlled trials.
- Teams record a first control decision, drive power/speed tuning decision, and next test.

Teacher Preparation:

- Prepare safe test area, batteries, basic inspection criteria, repair supplies, storage procedure, Driver Station/Robot Controller devices, D-pad and joystick demo OpModes, controller mapping comparison activity, and timing/measurement tools.

Student Background Reading / Preparation:

- `02_student_materials/readings/principles_of_robot_assembly_day3.md`
- `02_student_materials/readings/day3_session5_chassis_physics_space_background.md`
- `02_student_materials/readings/future_human_controller_mapping_background.md`
- `02_student_materials/decision_menus/controller_mapping_drive_comparison_activity.md`
- `02_student_materials/sld_prompts/day3_session6_controller_mapping_sld.md`
- `02_student_materials/sld_prompts/day3_session6_drive_power_tuning_sld.md`
- `02_student_materials/code_examples/onbot_java/DemoDriveDPad.java`

### Day 4, Session 8: Wire, Configure, Test Drive, and Round-Robin Challenge

Purpose: Connect physical wiring and robot configuration to Java TeleOp control, then use a two-robot timed challenge to test drive reliability, communication, and control decisions.

Success Criteria:

Teacher Preparation:

Student Background Reading / Preparation:

- `02_student_materials/readings/day4_session8_wiring_configuration_and_first_drive.md`
- `02_student_materials/readings/day4_session8_troubleshooting_basic_drive.md`
- `02_student_materials/guides/day4_session8_drive_reliability_round_robin_challenge.md`
- `02_student_materials/sld_prompts/reusable_robot_operations_role_plan_sld.md`
- `02_student_materials/sld_prompts/day3_session6_controller_mapping_sld.md`
- `02_student_materials/sld_prompts/day3_session6_drive_power_tuning_sld.md`
- Day 2 programming readings.

Student-Led Discussion / Decision:

- Troubleshooting prompt: What should we change first when the robot does not drive the way we expected?
- Robot Operations Role Plan SLD prompt: How should our team assign and practice robot operation roles so we are safe, fair, competitive, and prepared if someone is absent?
- Post-challenge reflection prompt: What did the driving challenge reveal about our chassis, controls, drive tuning, and driver communication?

Work Time Options:

- Wiring: connect motors and manage wires safely.
- Programming: confirm hardwareMap names and TeleOp.
- Testing: on-block test, then controlled floor test if safe.
- Challenge: ready teams run a two-robot round-robin timed course.
- Documentation: test table, code/config notes, race run record, adjustment log, and role decision record.

Differentiated Session Pathways:

| Team Status | Session 8 Pathway |
|---|---|
| Robot not safely driving | Wiring, configuration, chassis repair, first drive |
| Robot drives but is unreliable | Controller mapping, power tuning, practice laps |
| Robot drives reliably | Two-robot round-robin timed challenge |
| Advanced team | Revise tuning, rotate drivers, improve clean-run score |

Round-Robin Format:

| Round | Match |
|---|---|
| 1 | Team A vs Team B |
| 2 | Team C vs Team D |
| 3 | Team A vs Team C |
| 4 | Team B vs Team D |
| 5 | Team A vs Team D |
| 6 | Team B vs Team C |

Each team gets three runs if time allows. If time is short, use one practice run, one official timed run, and one improvement run if possible.

Course / Track Rules:

- Two robots start from marked starting zones.
- Both robots follow the same general route or complete the same loop.
- Robots must avoid cones/markers, boundaries, and each other.
- Drivers may need to pass, wait, or adjust speed.
- Unsafe driving results in a reset, penalty, stop, rerun, or disqualification at teacher discretion.
- The teacher may modify the course for space and safety.

This is a controlled driving challenge, not bumper cars. The goal is fast, clean, controlled driving.

Scoring:

```text
final score = time + penalties
```

| Event | Penalty |
|---|---|
| Touches cone / marker / boundary | +5 seconds |
| Needs a reset | +10 seconds |
| Robot-to-robot contact | +10 seconds |
| Unsafe driving | Stop, rerun, or disqualification at teacher discretion |

Between runs, teams should change only one or two things at a time and record what changed.

Must / Should / Could:

- Must: Test basic drivetrain motion safely with wheels lifted first.
- Should: Record at least one symptom, likely cause, check, change, and result.
- Could: Complete a two-robot timed challenge, rotate drivers, improve clean-run score, or revise tuning based on run evidence.

Deliverables:

- Code/config notes.
- Testing results.
- Wiring/config table.
- Race run record, if ready.
- Adjustment log, if ready.
- Drive-team role decision record.
- Engineering portfolio entry.

Engineering Portfolio Evidence:

- Motor port table, configuration names, Java variable names, test behavior, fixes, race times, penalties, adjustment log, role decision record, photo/video evidence if available, and one chassis/control/driver-practice revision.

Reflection / Share-Out:

- What bug or mismatch did the team diagnose?
- What adjustment did the team make between runs?
- What did the challenge reveal about chassis reliability, controls, tuning, or driver/operator communication?

Teacher Notes:

- Require students to stop OpModes before changing wires.
- Normalize debugging as engineering.

## 9. Co-Teacher Notes

Support teams without taking over:

- Ask questions before giving directions.
- Put tools in students' hands when it is safe.
- Invite quiet students into concrete tasks: count holes, take photos, hold parts, run the checklist, read code names, record test results.
- Ask experienced students to explain and then let someone else try.

Useful coaching questions:

- What problem are you trying to solve?
- What evidence supports that choice?
- What is the simplest prototype you can test?
- What should go in the engineering portfolio?
- What would you try next if this does not work?
- What is the smallest safe change you can make?
- How will you know whether this improved the robot?

Materials management:

- Begin and end each session with visible tool/part accountability.
- Require teams to report missing, damaged, or stripped parts honestly.
- Keep batteries, power switches, and moving mechanisms under clear safety expectations.
- Label team robots and prototypes.

Documentation support:

- Do not wait until the end of the course for portfolio work.
- Ask each team for one photo, one decision, one test result, or one next step before cleanup.
- Keep deliverables small enough that students can actually complete them.

Handling frustration:

- Normalize failure as testing evidence.
- Pause teams that are forcing parts, overtightening, or making many changes at once.
- Help teams simplify when the design threatens a working robot.

Supporting different experience levels:

- Treat low-floor work as real contribution.
- Use high-ceiling Super Challenges when students are ready for depth.
- Do not let challenge work isolate a student from the team.
- Rotate opportunities to build, code, test, document, and present.

Using Must / Should / Could:

- Must defines the minimum meaningful evidence for the session.
- Should defines the target if the team is on track.
- Could defines an extension or Super Challenge.
- A Could task is not automatically better if it risks safety, reliability, or team participation.
