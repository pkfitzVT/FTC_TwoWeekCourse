# Harkness Discussion Tool Impact Study Plan

## Purpose

This document outlines a lightweight impact study for a student-led discussion tool designed to support Harkness-style classroom conversations.

The tool records student discussion audio, transcribes the audio, classifies student contributions against a discussion rubric, generates feedback, and displays a Harkness-style circle graph showing participation patterns. The goal of the study is to determine whether students believe the tool improves student-led discussion, collaborative decision-making, equitable participation, and reflection.

This study is intended for classroom improvement and internal program evidence. If the results are later used for public research, publication, or external promotion, additional school/district approval and privacy review may be required.

## Core Question

> Does the Harkness discussion tool help students improve student-led discussion, collaborative decision-making, equitable participation, listening, and reflection?

## Why This Matters

The robotics course is built around real engineering work. Students must make decisions as teams, explain design choices, respond to failure, revise ideas, and share expertise. A Harkness-style discussion tool may help students see the patterns in their collaboration that are often invisible during group work.

The tool is not intended to replace teacher judgment or student reflection. It is intended to provide evidence students can use to improve the quality of their discussions and team decisions.

## Tool Description

The discussion tool currently includes or is expected to include:

- Audio recording of student discussion
- Speech-to-text transcription
- Classification of contributions against a discussion rubric
- Rubric-based score or feedback
- A Harkness-style circle graph showing participation patterns
- Student or teacher reflection based on the data

## Constructs to Measure

### 1. Student-Led Discussion

Measures whether students feel more capable of managing discussion without the teacher controlling every step.

Possible indicators:

- Students initiate ideas
- Students respond to each other
- Students ask questions
- Students build on prior comments
- Students reference evidence
- Students move the group toward a decision

### 2. Collaborative Decision-Making

Measures whether the tool helps teams make better decisions together.

Possible indicators:

- More voices included
- More evidence used
- Disagreements handled productively
- Decisions are explained clearly
- Teams reflect on how decisions were made

### 3. Equitable Participation

Measures whether students notice and improve participation balance.

Possible indicators:

- Quieter students are invited in
- Dominant speakers become more aware
- Students notice who has not spoken
- The circle graph helps teams reflect on participation

### 4. Discussion Quality

Measures whether students understand what makes a strong discussion.

Possible indicators:

- Listening
- Questioning
- Building on ideas
- Using evidence
- Clarifying confusion
- Summarizing group thinking
- Respectful disagreement

### 5. Trust, Fairness, and Safety

Measures whether students experience the tool as supportive rather than surveillance-based.

Possible indicators:

- Students understand what is being recorded
- Students know who can see the data
- Students believe the tool's feedback is useful
- Students can challenge or reflect on the classification
- Students do not feel publicly shamed by the data

## Suggested Survey Schedule

Use the same three-point rhythm as the robotics impact study.

| Course Point | Timing | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Survey 1 | Day 1 | Baseline snapshot of how students currently experience group discussion and decision-making |
| Survey 2 | Day 5 | Mid-course pulse after students have used the tool and experienced team decision-making |
| Survey 3 | Day 9 | Near-final impact survey before final presentations |

## Where to Add Tool Study Activities in Lesson Plans

### Day 1 / Session 1

Add a short baseline survey after the course culture discussion or before the first formal team activity.

Suggested lesson-plan note:

> Students complete the Discussion and Collaboration Baseline Survey. This survey asks about student comfort with speaking, listening, building on ideas, making decisions in groups, and participating in student-led discussions. Explain that the survey is not graded and will be used to help the teachers understand student starting points.

### Day 5 / Session 9 or 10

Add a midpoint pulse survey after students have used the tool at least once or twice.

Suggested lesson-plan note:

> Students complete the Discussion Tool Midpoint Pulse Survey. This survey asks whether the tool helped them notice participation patterns, reflect on team decisions, and improve the quality of discussion.

### Day 9 / Session 17 or 18

Add a near-final survey after students have enough experience with the tool and before final presentations.

Suggested lesson-plan note:

> Students complete the Discussion Tool Impact Survey. This survey repeats baseline questions and asks students to describe how the tool affected their participation, listening, collaborative decision-making, and confidence in student-led discussion.

## Recommended Use Guidelines

The tool should be introduced as a reflection tool, not a surveillance or punishment tool.

Suggested teacher framing:

> This tool is not here to catch you or rank you as people. It is here to help the group see patterns that are usually invisible: who is talking, who is being heard, whether people are building on ideas, asking questions, using evidence, and making decisions together.

Recommended classroom norms:

- Use the tool for reflection before using it for evaluation.
- Show team-level patterns before individual-level scores.
- Allow students to respond to or challenge the classification.
- Avoid public shaming based on participation data.
- Make clear what is recorded, what is stored, who can see it, and when it will be deleted.
- Provide students with non-recorded practice discussions before recorded discussions.
- If possible, let students use code names or team identifiers during early testing.

## Evidence to Collect

### Quantitative Evidence

- Change in student comfort with speaking
- Change in student confidence with listening and building on ideas
- Change in student confidence with group decision-making
- Change in perceived participation balance
- Percent of students who say the tool helped their team make better decisions
- Percent of students who say the tool felt supportive
- Percent of students who would recommend using the tool again

### Qualitative Evidence

- Student quotes about discussion growth
- Student quotes about team decision-making
- Student quotes about what the circle graph revealed
- Student quotes about fairness and trust
- Student suggestions for improvement

### Tool Data Evidence

Use caution with tool-generated data. For internal review, the following may be useful:

- Number of speakers
- Participation distribution
- Discussion-move categories
- Team-level rubric trends
- Changes over time in discussion quality
- Examples of productive decision-making moments

For admin-facing reports, use de-identified or aggregated data unless explicit permission has been obtained.
