# Robotics Course Impact Study Plan

## Purpose

This document outlines a lightweight impact study for the 20-session robotics course. The purpose is to document whether the course increases student curiosity, confidence, collaboration, STEM identity, and interest in participating in FIRST Tech Challenge or a future school-based FIRST program.

This is not intended to be a formal human-subjects research study. It is intended as a classroom/program-improvement evidence system that can help the teaching team reflect on the course and provide credible evidence to school administrators when proposing a FIRST robotics program.

## Core Claim to Investigate

The robotics course is designed to give students a real, team-based engineering experience where they apply math, science, computer science, design, communication, and collaborative decision-making to a messy problem with no guaranteed outcome.

The impact study asks:

> Does participation in this robotics course increase students' confidence, curiosity, STEM identity, collaborative decision-making, and interest in joining or supporting a FIRST robotics program?

## Why This Matters

FIRST-style robotics experiences are valuable because they ask students to solve open-ended problems in teams. Students must build, program, test, revise, communicate, and make decisions under uncertainty. This connects school subjects to real-world problem solving, invention, product development, entrepreneurship, and technical careers.

The study should help answer three practical questions:

1. Did the course increase student interest in STEM, robotics, coding, engineering, or design?
2. Did students develop confidence in collaborative problem-solving and technical learning?
3. Did the course create enough student interest and evidence of value to support starting a school FIRST program?

## Research and Evidence Base

This study is loosely informed by the Student Attitudes toward STEM (S-STEM) survey framework, which measures changes in students' confidence and efficacy in STEM subjects, 21st-century learning skills, and interest in STEM careers.

FIRST also publishes impact evidence showing long-term effects on STEM engagement, career interest, and college persistence among FIRST alumni. FIRST also maintains scholarship and alumni resources that may be relevant when explaining the value of a school-based FIRST program.

Suggested evidence sources for future admin-facing documents:

- Friday Institute for Educational Innovation. Student Attitudes toward STEM (S-STEM) Survey.
- FIRST Impact / FIRST Longitudinal Study.
- FIRST Scholarship Program and FIRST Alumni resources.

## Study Design

Use three student surveys during the course:

| Course Point | Timing | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Survey 1 | Day 1 | Baseline snapshot of student attitudes, confidence, interests, and concerns |
| Survey 2 | Day 5 | Mid-course pulse check after teams have begun building/testing and have experienced productive struggle |
| Survey 3 | Day 9 | Near-final snapshot before Day 10 public presentations, capturing change, evidence of learning, and interest in FIRST |

Day 10 is reserved for final presentations to other courses. If there is time after presentations, students may complete a short final reflection, but the primary post-course evidence should be collected on Day 9.

## Where to Add Surveys in the Lesson Plans

### Day 1 / Session 1: Baseline Survey

Add to the lesson plan:

> Students complete the Robotics Course Baseline Survey. This survey captures students' starting attitudes toward math, science, coding, engineering, teamwork, productive struggle, and interest in FIRST/robotics. Explain that this is not a test and that honest answers will help the teachers support teams and measure the impact of the course.

Suggested placement: end of Session 1 after course culture discussion and before final reflection.

### Day 5 / Session 9 or 10: Mid-Course Pulse Survey

Add to the lesson plan:

> Students complete a short mid-course pulse survey to document how their confidence, team experience, and interest are changing. This survey should be completed after students have encountered real design challenges, testing problems, or moments of uncertainty.

Suggested placement: end of Day 5, after flywheel construction basics and/or projectile motion work.

### Day 9 / Session 17 or 18: Near-Final Impact Survey

Add to the lesson plan:

> Students complete the near-final course impact survey before the final presentation day. This survey repeats key baseline questions and asks students to describe evidence of growth, teamwork, problem-solving, and interest in future FIRST participation.

Suggested placement: end of Session 18, after practice challenge/evidence collection and before final presentation planning is complete.

## Main Constructs to Measure

The surveys should measure the following categories.

### 1. STEM Curiosity and Interest

Measures whether the course increases curiosity about STEM fields and future learning.

Possible indicators:

- Interest in robotics
- Interest in engineering
- Interest in coding
- Interest in math/science applications
- Curiosity about how machines, apps, or products are made

### 2. STEM Confidence and Identity

Measures whether students begin to see themselves as capable of technical learning.

Possible indicators:

- “I can learn technical ideas”
- “I can use math or science to solve real problems”
- “I can imagine myself doing robotics/coding/engineering/design”
- “I can contribute even if I am not already an expert”

### 3. Collaborative Decision-Making

Measures whether students grow in their ability to work with others on open-ended problems.

Possible indicators:

- Listening to teammates
- Sharing expertise
- Explaining ideas
- Making team decisions
- Learning from other teams
- Giving and receiving help

### 4. Productive Struggle and Engineering Mindset

Measures whether students develop comfort with uncertainty, failure, testing, and revision.

Possible indicators:

- Comfort with problems that do not have answer keys
- Persistence when a design fails
- Willingness to revise ideas
- Ability to learn from mistakes
- Understanding that chaos and uncertainty are part of engineering

### 5. Interest in FIRST Participation

Measures whether the course builds support for a future school FIRST program.

Possible indicators:

- Interest in joining a FIRST team
- Interest in attending a robotics meeting or demonstration
- Interest in building, programming, driving, documenting, or presenting
- Interest in helping start a school team
- Interest in learning more about FIRST opportunities, scholarships, competitions, or careers

## Survey Structure

Use Google Forms for all surveys. Use mostly 1–5 Likert-scale questions, repeated across Survey 1 and Survey 3, with a few open-response questions.

Scale:

1. Strongly disagree  
2. Disagree  
3. Not sure  
4. Agree  
5. Strongly agree  

## Survey 1: Day 1 Baseline Survey

### Suggested Title

Robotics Course Baseline Survey

### Suggested Description

This is not a test and there are no wrong answers. Mrs. Probric and Mr. Fitzgerald will use this survey to understand your starting point, support teams, and measure how the course changes students' confidence, interests, and collaboration skills.

### Likert-Scale Questions

1. I can use math to help solve real problems.
2. I can use science ideas to explain how something works.
3. I can learn to write or modify code.
4. I can help design or improve a mechanical system.
5. I can contribute to a team even if I am not the expert.
6. I can explain my ideas to other people.
7. I can learn from people who know more than I do.
8. I can help another student learn something I understand.
9. When something does not work the first time, I can keep trying.
10. I can handle a class where there is not always one clear answer.
11. I can learn from mistakes without feeling like I failed.
12. I can imagine myself doing robotics, coding, engineering, science, or design in the future.
13. I understand how school subjects can connect to real-world problem solving.
14. I am interested in learning how people create products, solve problems, or build businesses.
15. I would be interested in learning more about FIRST robotics.
16. I might be interested in participating in a FIRST robotics team or robotics club.

### Open-Response Questions

1. When you hear “robotics,” what do you think of?
2. What is one strength you bring to a team?
3. What is one thing that might be challenging for you in this course?
4. What would make this course feel successful for you?
5. Right now, do you see yourself as a math/science/coding/engineering person? Why or why not?

## Survey 2: Day 5 Mid-Course Pulse Survey

### Suggested Title

Robotics Course Midpoint Pulse Check

### Suggested Description

This survey helps us understand how the course is going so far. Please answer honestly. Your responses will help us support teams and improve the course.

### Likert-Scale Questions

1. I understand my role on my team.
2. My team is getting better at making decisions together.
3. I have contributed something useful to my team.
4. I have learned something from another student.
5. I have helped another student learn something.
6. I am more comfortable with robotics than I was at the start of the course.
7. I am more interested in robotics, coding, engineering, science, or design than I was at the start.
8. I understand that testing and failure are part of engineering.
9. When our robot or design does not work, my team can figure out a next step.
10. I can see how math, science, or coding connects to what we are building.

### Open-Response Questions

1. What is one thing your team has figured out so far?
2. What is one problem your team has faced?
3. How did your team respond when something did not work?
4. What is one thing you personally contributed?
5. What does your team need next?
6. Are you more, less, or equally interested in FIRST robotics compared with the start of the course? Explain.

## Survey 3: Day 9 Near-Final Impact Survey

### Suggested Title

Robotics Course Impact Survey

### Suggested Description

This survey helps us understand what students learned from the robotics course and whether the course increased interest in STEM, engineering, coding, collaboration, and FIRST robotics. Please answer honestly and include specific examples when possible.

### Repeat Likert-Scale Questions from Survey 1

Use the same questions from the baseline survey so that beginning and near-final responses can be compared.

1. I can use math to help solve real problems.
2. I can use science ideas to explain how something works.
3. I can learn to write or modify code.
4. I can help design or improve a mechanical system.
5. I can contribute to a team even if I am not the expert.
6. I can explain my ideas to other people.
7. I can learn from people who know more than I do.
8. I can help another student learn something I understand.
9. When something does not work the first time, I can keep trying.
10. I can handle a class where there is not always one clear answer.
11. I can learn from mistakes without feeling like I failed.
12. I can imagine myself doing robotics, coding, engineering, science, or design in the future.
13. I understand how school subjects can connect to real-world problem solving.
14. I am interested in learning how people create products, solve problems, or build businesses.
15. I would be interested in learning more about FIRST robotics.
16. I might be interested in participating in a FIRST robotics team or robotics club.

### Additional Near-Final Questions

1. This course helped me understand what engineering feels like.
2. This course helped me understand how teams solve real problems.
3. This course made me more interested in taking another STEM, coding, robotics, engineering, or design course.
4. I would recommend that the school offer more robotics opportunities.
5. I would be interested in attending an informational meeting about a future FIRST robotics team.

### Open-Response Questions

1. What is one way your thinking about robotics, coding, engineering, math, or science changed?
2. What is one problem your team faced, and how did your team respond?
3. What is one piece of evidence that shows your growth during the course?
4. What did you learn about working on a team?
5. What did you learn about making decisions with other people?
6. What did you learn about dealing with uncertainty, failure, or frustration?
7. Are you interested in participating in FIRST robotics or a robotics club in the future? Why or why not?
8. What would you tell an administrator about whether this school should have a FIRST robotics program?

## Optional Day 10 Presentation Evidence

If time allows, collect one quick final reflection after presentations:

1. What did you learn from presenting your robot or project to other students?
2. What did the audience notice or ask about?
3. What are you most proud of from this course?
4. What would you improve if you had one more week?

If there is no time on Day 10, these questions can be assigned as a brief follow-up form or reflection.

## Evidence Beyond Surveys

To make the admin pitch stronger, collect multiple kinds of evidence.

### Quantitative Evidence

- Average change from Survey 1 to Survey 3
- Percent of students who increased in STEM interest
- Percent of students who increased in confidence with coding/engineering
- Percent of students interested in learning more about FIRST
- Percent of students interested in joining a robotics team or club
- Percent of students who recommend that the school offer more robotics opportunities

### Qualitative Evidence

- Student quotes about growth
- Student quotes about teamwork
- Student quotes about frustration and persistence
- Student quotes about math/science/coding connections
- Student quotes about interest in FIRST

### Portfolio Evidence

- Team engineering bios
- Design sketches
- Build photos
- Code screenshots
- Test data
- Reflection entries
- Presentation slides/scripts
- Final robot demonstration evidence
- Evidence of iteration: first design, failed test, revision, improved design

### Teacher Observation Evidence

Use a simple teacher observation log once or twice per week:

| Date | Team | Evidence of collaboration | Evidence of technical learning | Evidence of productive struggle | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|

## Suggested Data Analysis

### Simple Quantitative Analysis

For each repeated Likert item:

1. Calculate class average on Day 1.
2. Calculate class average on Day 9.
3. Calculate change: Day 9 average minus Day 1 average.
4. Identify strongest growth areas.
5. Identify areas that need improvement in the course design.

Example table:

| Construct | Day 1 Avg | Day 9 Avg | Change |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| STEM confidence | 3.1 | 4.0 | +0.9 |
| Coding confidence | 2.4 | 3.6 | +1.2 |
| Engineering identity | 2.8 | 3.9 | +1.1 |
| Collaborative decision-making | 3.4 | 4.2 | +0.8 |
| Productive struggle | 3.0 | 3.8 | +0.8 |
| Interest in FIRST | 2.6 | 3.9 | +1.3 |

### Simple Qualitative Analysis

Code open responses using a small set of themes:

- STEM curiosity
- Confidence
- Teamwork
- Leadership
- Coding interest
- Mechanical/building interest
- Productive struggle
- Engineering identity
- FIRST interest
- Future course/program interest

Use representative student quotes in an admin-facing summary.

## Admin-Facing Summary Structure

At the end of the course, create a short report with the following sections:

1. Course Overview
2. Why Robotics / Why FIRST
3. Student Survey Results
4. Evidence of Student Growth
5. Student Quotes
6. Portfolio and Demonstration Evidence
7. Interest in Future FIRST Participation
8. Resource Needs for a FIRST Program
9. Recommendation

## Possible Admin Pitch Claim

> The 20-session robotics course increased student interest in STEM, built confidence in collaborative technical problem-solving, and generated student interest in FIRST robotics. Based on survey evidence, student reflections, and final project demonstrations, a school-based FIRST program would provide a natural extension for students who want deeper engineering, coding, leadership, and teamwork opportunities.

## Data Privacy and Ethics Notes

Because this study is intended for classroom improvement and internal program planning:

- Do not publish student names with survey responses.
- Use anonymous or de-identified quotes in admin reports unless students give explicit permission.
- Explain to students that honest answers are valuable and that survey responses are not graded.
- If sharing photos or videos outside the classroom, follow school media permission policies.
- If the study becomes formal research or is shared publicly, consult school/district guidelines.
