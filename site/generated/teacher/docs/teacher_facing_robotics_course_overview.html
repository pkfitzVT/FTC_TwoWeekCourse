Course Purpose

This course uses robotics as an entry point into advanced STEM learning. Students work in teams to design, build, program, test, and present a robot while connecting their work to engineering, computer science, mathematics, physics, data analysis, careers, and their own strengths. The course emphasizes curiosity, collaboration, risk-taking, iteration, and student-led problem solving.

This is a 10-day, 20-session modified FIRST Tech Challenge course. It is an invitation into robotics team participation and advanced STEM coursework, not a compressed replacement for a full FTC season. Students use FTC-style parts, Java programming ideas, engineering notebooks, inspection habits, testing evidence, and presentation practice to see how robotics connects to AP Computer Science A, AP Physics, AP Calculus, AP Statistics, engineering, design, programming, fabrication, data analysis, and technical communication.

The course culture is low-floor and high-ceiling. Every student should participate in building, testing, coding, documenting, and presenting in some way. Challenge by Choice pathways give students ways to go deeper without locking them into permanent roles.

Course Design Principles

  • Student-led problem solving
  • Modified FIRST Tech Challenge structure
  • Teams of 4-5 students
  • Engineering portfolio documentation
  • Robot demonstration
  • Must / Should / Could framework
  • Optional Super Challenges
  • Productive risk-taking and iteration
  • Read-discuss-design-build-test-document-reflect cycle
  • Connections to STEM careers and coursework

Typical Session Routine

Most sessions may include:

1. Statement of purpose or learning goal 2. Background reading or technical preparation 3. Student-led discussion 4. Design choice menu or team decision 5. Work time 6. Fabrication, programming, testing, data analysis, or documentation 7. Deliverables check 8. Engineering portfolio update 9. Group reflection or share-out

Not every session includes every component. A session may focus mostly on assembly, programming, testing, documentation, or presentation. The routine is meant to keep the work purposeful, not to create paperwork for its own sake.

Deliverables System

Students should produce useful evidence during each session. Common deliverable types include:

  • SLD document: student-led discussion record showing the team's decision-making process.
  • Design drawing: sketch, diagram, CAD screenshot, labeled mechanism drawing, or annotated plan.
  • Build photos: photos of the chassis, mechanism, prototype, wiring, sensor placement, or test setup.
  • Code: any OpMode, function, pseudocode, commented code block, configuration notes, or code revision.
  • Testing results: data table, observations, failed test notes, successful test notes, measurements, driver feedback, or next-step decisions.
  • Engineering portfolio entry: a short written entry explaining what the team did, what decision they made, what evidence they used, and what they need to do next.

The SLD document captures the team's decision-making process. The engineering portfolio is the final organized record of the team's work. Students may create separate SLD notes, sketches, photos, code, and test results during the session, but the important evidence should eventually be summarized or linked in the engineering portfolio.

Assessment / Products

Student teams produce:

  • Engineering portfolio
  • Robot demonstration
  • Team reflection
  • Evidence of design process
  • Evidence of testing and revision
  • Optional Super Challenge evidence

Assessment should value safety, contribution, evidence, iteration, communication, and growth. A robot that works imperfectly but is tested honestly and explained clearly can still show strong engineering learning.

Co-Teacher Notes

Support teams without taking over:

  • Ask questions before giving directions.
  • Put tools in students' hands when it is safe.
  • Invite quiet students into concrete tasks such as counting holes, taking photos, holding parts, running the checklist, reading code names, or recording test results.
  • Ask experienced students to explain and then let someone else try.

Useful coaching questions:

  • What problem are you trying to solve?
  • What evidence supports that choice?
  • What is the simplest prototype you can test?
  • What should go in the engineering portfolio?
  • What would you try next if this does not work?
  • What is the smallest safe change you can make?
  • How will you know whether this improved the robot?

Use Must / Should / Could to differentiate:

  • Must defines the minimum meaningful evidence for the session.
  • Should defines the target if the team is on track.
  • Could defines an extension or Super Challenge.
  • A Could task is not automatically better if it risks safety, reliability, or team participation.

Manage frustration by normalizing failed tests, slowing teams down when they force parts or change too many variables, and helping teams simplify when complexity threatens a working robot.

Manage materials by beginning and ending each session with visible tool/part accountability, requiring honest reporting of damaged or missing parts, labeling team builds, and keeping batteries, power switches, and moving mechanisms under clear safety expectations.

Open Planning Questions

  • Which exact game/challenge rules and scoring sheets will be used for Sessions 17 and 20?
  • Are teams building from identical chassis kits, recycled chassis, or fully team-selected materials?
  • What materials, motors, wheels, hubs, servos, brackets, batteries, and tools are available per team?
  • What is the exact format for SLD documents after Session 1?
  • Where should students store photos, code, testing results, and portfolio entries during the two weeks?
  • Which readings will be printed in packets versus accessed digitally?
  • Which Super Challenges are officially offered, and which are draft extension ideas?
  • What final presentation rubric or judging form should be used?
  • Will final awards use course-friendly categories only, or any official FTC-style award names already approved by the instructor?
  • How much autonomous or scripted behavior is expected, given the newer course arc emphasizes drivetrain, flywheel, hopper, trigger, inspection, and presentation?
