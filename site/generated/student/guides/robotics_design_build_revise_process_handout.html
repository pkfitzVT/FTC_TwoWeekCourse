Design, Build, and Revise

A Natural Robotics Design Process

In this course, your team will design and build a robot using real materials, real constraints, and limited time.

Your first idea will probably not fit perfectly. A part may not line up. A connector may be unavailable. A wheel, motor, servo, or bracket may block the space where another part needs to go.

That is not a failure.

That is engineering.

The goal is not to force the robot to match your first sketch. The goal is to move from a rough idea toward a working design by using the parts, space, tools, code, and evidence available.

---

The Big Idea

Your first sketch explains the idea. Your final robot explains what your team learned.

A good design process moves through stages:

1. Rough concept 2. Available materials 3. Specific geometry 4. Connector and mounting decisions 5. Space and interference checks 6. Build and test 7. Revise based on evidence

---

Step 1: Start With a Rough Concept Sketch

Do not begin by grabbing random parts and attaching them together.

Start with a rough sketch that explains the main idea.

Your sketch does not need to be beautiful. It needs to communicate the design goal.

Your rough sketch should show

  • What the mechanism is supposed to do.
  • Where the major parts might go.
  • How the object, ball, arm, wheel, or mechanism might move.
  • What direction forces or motion might happen.
  • Where motors, servos, wheels, axles, or supports might be located.
  • What part of the robot this design connects to.

Good rough-sketch questions

  • What problem are we trying to solve?
  • What is the basic motion we want?
  • What part moves?
  • What part stays still?
  • What does the driver or operator need the robot to do?
  • What would success look like?

Remember

The first sketch is not a blueprint. It is a conversation starter.

---

Step 2: Check What Materials Are Available

After your team has a rough idea, look at the actual materials.

This is where the design becomes more realistic.

Check for

  • Available beams, channels, brackets, plates, or wood.
  • Motors and servos.
  • Wheels, shafts, bearings, hubs, or collars.
  • Screws, nuts, spacers, and connectors.
  • Sensors.
  • Wires and ports.
  • Tools.
  • Shared-library parts that may or may not be available.

Ask

  • Do we actually have the parts our sketch assumes?
  • Is there a simpler part that can do the same job?
  • Can we substitute something from the starter kit?
  • Do we need to redesign around the parts we have?
  • Is this part worth using, or should we save it for a more important function?

Important mindset

A missing part is not the end of a design. It is the beginning of a design decision.

---

Step 3: Refine the Design Based on Real Parts

Now revise the sketch using the materials you actually have.

This is where the design starts moving from idea to buildable plan.

Refine the design by deciding

  • Which motor or servo will be used.
  • Where the mechanism will attach to the robot.
  • What parts will support the load.
  • How the moving part will rotate, slide, lift, push, or guide.
  • What fasteners or brackets will connect the pieces.
  • How the mechanism will be controlled by code.
  • What needs to be measured before building.

Add details to the sketch

  • Hole locations.
  • Axle positions.
  • Motor location.
  • Servo location.
  • Support points.
  • Direction of motion.
  • Expected range of movement.
  • Areas where other parts might get in the way.

Remember

The design should become more specific only after your team checks the actual materials.

---

Step 4: Check Connectors and Physical Geometry

Many beginner designs fail because the idea is good, but the parts do not physically connect the way the team imagined.

FTC-style parts have real hole patterns, real brackets, real shaft sizes, real screw lengths, and real geometry.

Check before building

  • Do the holes line up?
  • Can a screw actually reach?
  • Is there room for a nut, washer, collar, or spacer?
  • Can the wrench or hex key reach the fastener?
  • Does the motor mount match the hole pattern?
  • Does the axle have support on both sides?
  • Is the shaft long enough?
  • Is the servo horn in the correct position?
  • Can the moving part rotate without hitting the frame?
  • Will the part bend, twist, wobble, or jam?

Useful strategy

Before permanently attaching parts:

1. Hold the parts in place. 2. Check the holes. 3. Finger-tighten first. 4. Move the mechanism by hand. 5. Look for rubbing, bending, or blocked motion. 6. Then tighten or revise.

Remember

A design is not buildable until the connectors, holes, shafts, and supports actually work together.

---

Step 5: Check Space Management and Interference

A robot is a crowded system. One part may block another part.

A good design does not only ask, “Can this part work by itself?”

It asks:

Can this part work while everything else is also on the robot?

Check for space conflicts

  • Does the mechanism block the drivetrain?
  • Does it block access to the battery?
  • Does it block wires or ports?
  • Does it block another mechanism?
  • Does it block the driver’s view or the robot’s movement?
  • Does it make the robot too wide, too tall, or too hard to carry?
  • Does it make repairs difficult?
  • Does a moving part swing into another part?
  • Does the ball, arm, wheel, or trigger have a clear path?

Useful space-management questions

  • What parts must stay accessible?
  • What parts need room to move?
  • What parts need to be protected?
  • What parts should not be removed every time we make a repair?
  • What will happen when the robot drives, turns, bumps, or vibrates?

Remember

A robot is a system. A part that works alone may fail when it is added to the whole robot.

---

Step 6: Watch for Cascading Design Decisions

One design choice often changes many other parts of the robot.

This is called a cascade.

For example:

  • Moving a motor may change where the wires run.
  • Changing a wheel size may change the height of the mechanism.
  • Moving an axle may require a different support.
  • Adding a hopper may block access to the battery.
  • Changing the shooter angle may require a new side plate.
  • Changing a servo position may require new code.
  • Adding a sensor may require a bracket, wiring, telemetry, and testing.
  • Choosing a large part may make the robot harder to drive.

Before making a major change, ask

  • What else will this change affect?
  • Will the code need to change?
  • Will the wiring need to change?
  • Will another mechanism be blocked?
  • Will this make the robot easier or harder to repair?
  • Will this make testing easier or harder?
  • Will this make the robot more reliable or just more complicated?

Remember

Every part decision has consequences. Good engineers think about the chain reaction before they build.

---

Step 7: Build a Small Version First When Possible

Before changing the full robot, test the idea in a small way.

Prototype options

  • Hold parts in place by hand.
  • Use cardboard or paper templates.
  • Attach parts temporarily.
  • Test the mechanism off the robot.
  • Move the mechanism by hand before adding power.
  • Test one motor or servo at a time.
  • Build only one side before duplicating it.

Ask

  • What is the smallest test that would tell us if this idea might work?
  • Can we test the motion before fully mounting it?
  • Can we test the code before the mechanism is complete?
  • Can we test the spacing before cutting or drilling?

Remember

Fast tests prevent slow failures.

---

Step 8: Build, Test, Observe, Revise

Once the design is mounted, test it.

Do not just ask whether it works. Ask how well it works.

Test for

  • Reliability.
  • Consistency.
  • Strength.
  • Smooth motion.
  • Driver control.
  • Ease of repair.
  • Safety.
  • Code response.
  • Repeatability.

Record

  • What you tested.
  • What happened.
  • What failed.
  • What changed.
  • What evidence shows improvement.

The engineering cycle

Build → Test → Observe → Discuss → Revise → Test Again

Remember

A good design changes because the team learns from evidence.

---

Step 9: Protect the Working Robot

Exploration is important, but your team should always know the last version of the robot that worked.

Before making a major change:

  • Take a photo.
  • Record what currently works.
  • Discuss what might break.
  • Make a plan for how to test the change.
  • Keep track of parts that are removed.
  • Be able to explain how to return to a working version if needed.

Remember

You are allowed to experiment, but your team is responsible for keeping a working version of the robot alive.

---

Design Process Checklist

Before building, can your team answer these questions?

Concept

  • What problem are we solving?
  • What is the basic idea?
  • What motion or function do we need?

Materials

  • What parts do we have?
  • What parts are unavailable?
  • What substitutions are possible?

Geometry

  • Do the holes line up?
  • Is the shaft long enough?
  • Can the fasteners reach?
  • Can the tool reach the fasteners?

Space

  • What might this part block?
  • What needs room to move?
  • What needs to stay accessible?

Cascade

  • What else changes if we choose this design?
  • Does the code need to change?
  • Does the wiring need to change?
  • Does another mechanism need to move?

Test

  • What is the smallest useful test?
  • What data should we record?
  • How will we know whether the design improved?

---

Final Course Message

Your team is not expected to know the perfect design at the beginning.

Your team is expected to:

Sketch the idea. Check the parts. Refine the design. Manage the geometry. Watch for conflicts. Build carefully. Test honestly. Revise with evidence.

That is engineering.
