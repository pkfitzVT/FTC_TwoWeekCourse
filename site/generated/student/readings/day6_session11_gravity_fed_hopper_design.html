Session 11 Reading: Gravity-Fed Hopper Design

Feeding the Shooter

The hopper stores balls and guides them toward the shooter. It does not launch the ball. Its job is to place the ball in the right position so the flywheel can launch it consistently.

A gravity-fed system uses gravity to move balls downhill toward the trigger. Gravity-fed systems are often simpler than powered systems, but they still need careful design.

Hopper Vocabulary

A hopper is a container or guide that holds balls before they enter the shooter.

Gravity-fed means the balls move because they roll or slide downward, not because a motor pushes them the whole way.

The ball path is the route a ball takes through the hopper and into the shooter.

The feed path is the part of the ball path that moves balls toward the trigger or flywheel.

Loading means placing balls into the hopper or moving a ball into the ready position.

A trigger is the part that controls when a ball is released into the flywheel.

A release is the moment the trigger lets a ball move forward.

A guide rail is a side piece that keeps balls moving along the intended path.

A ramp is a sloped surface that helps balls roll or slide downward.

A slope is how steep a ramp or hopper surface is.

Friction is rubbing between the ball and the hopper surface. Some friction is normal, but too much can stop the ball.

A jam happens when a ball gets stuck and stops feeding.

Clearance is the space around the ball. Too little clearance can pinch the ball, and too much can let it move unpredictably.

Alignment means parts are lined up so the ball enters the shooter in the intended position.

Repeatability means the hopper feeds the ball the same way more than once.

Reliability means the hopper works many times without jamming or needing constant repair.

A prototype is an early version of a design built so the team can learn from it.

An iteration is a changed version made after testing.

Predictable Position Matters

A hopper should guide balls into a predictable position. The ball should not arrive at the flywheel randomly, sideways, too high, too low, or too fast.

If the ball enters the flywheel differently each time, the shot may change even if the motor power stays the same.

What Makes a Good Hopper?

A good hopper:

  • lets balls roll or slide smoothly toward the trigger,
  • holds more than one ball,
  • lets the bottom ball separate from the stack,
  • makes the ball path easy to see and troubleshoot,
  • does not squeeze the balls too tightly,
  • avoids too much rubbing against the sides,
  • leaves space for the flywheel, battery, Control Hub, wires, and driver access,
  • and can be adjusted after testing.

Simple is useful. A hopper that is easy to see and fix is usually better than a complicated hopper that hides the problem.

Why Feed One Ball at a Time?

The flywheel loses some speed when it launches a ball. It needs a short time to recover speed before the next shot.

When the flywheel launches a ball, the ball takes energy from the spinning wheel. The wheel slows down for a moment. If the next ball enters too soon, the wheel may not be back up to speed yet.

If multiple balls enter the flywheel too quickly, the shots may become weak, inconsistent, or jammed.

A hopper and trigger should help the shooter feed one ball at a time.

Reflection

How can your hopper help the shooter feed only one ball at a time?
