Session 10 Reading: Projectile Motion for Shooters

After the Ball Leaves the Shooter

Once the ball leaves the shooter, it becomes a projectile. The robot is no longer pushing it. The ball keeps moving forward while gravity pulls it down.

Your team does not need heavy equations to begin testing. You do need to understand how launch angle, speed, distance, and height work together.

Projectile Vocabulary

A projectile is an object moving through the air after it has been launched.

Projectile motion is the curved motion of a launched object as it moves forward and falls under gravity.

The launch angle is the angle the ball leaves the shooter.

Initial velocity is the ball's speed and direction at the moment it leaves the shooter.

Horizontal velocity is the forward part of the ball's motion.

Vertical velocity is the upward or downward part of the ball's motion.

Gravity is the force that pulls the ball downward during the entire flight.

A trajectory is the path the ball follows through the air.

An arc is a curved path.

Range is how far the ball travels forward.

Height is how high the ball is above the floor.

Distance is how far the robot is from the target.

A target is what the robot is trying to score into or hit.

Goal height is the height of the opening or scoring area.

Shooter height is the height where the ball leaves the robot.

Time of flight is how long the ball stays in the air.

An overshoot happens when the ball goes past or over the target.

An undershoot happens when the ball falls short of the target.

The Ball Moves Forward and Falls

The shooter gives the ball an initial velocity. Part of that motion is forward, and part may be upward.

Gravity pulls downward during the entire flight. Even if the ball starts by rising, gravity is already pulling it down.

Launch angle changes the shape of the path. A higher angle gives the ball more upward motion and a steeper arc. A lower angle gives a flatter shot.

A flatter shot may need more speed and may hit the goal harder.

Distance and Height Matter

If the robot is farther from the target, the ball usually needs more launch speed, a different launch angle, or both.

Shooter height and goal height also matter. A shooter that starts closer to the goal height may need less vertical motion. A lower shooter may need more upward angle or more speed.

Your team is choosing a relationship, not one magic number. Launch angle, distance, and initial velocity work together.

Comparing Shot Styles

Launch Style Possible Advantage Possible Problem
Higher arc Ball may drop into the goal more gently May need more space and can be sensitive to angle
Flatter shot Fast and direct May hit hard and bounce or skip out
More speed Travels farther Can create more bounce and more variation
Less speed Gentler impact May fall short

What Teams Can Adjust

Teams may adjust:

  • motor power,
  • wheel speed,
  • launch angle,
  • robot distance from the goal,
  • compression gap,
  • wheel choice,
  • and ball path through the shooter.

A Good Shooter Is Reliable, Not Just Powerful

The goal is not always the fastest shot. The goal is the shot that scores repeatedly from the position your robot can reach.

If your robot can reliably drive to one position, design and test the shooter for that position first.

Reflection

Would your team rather have a high arcing shot or a flatter direct shot? Why?

What distance from the goal does your team want to design for first?
