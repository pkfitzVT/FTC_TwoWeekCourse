Session 9 Reading: Flywheel Build and Test Planning

Build Something You Can Test

Today's goal is not to create a perfect shooter immediately. The goal is to build a testable shooter and learn from evidence.

A testable shooter lets your team change one thing, try a shot safely, record what happened, and decide what to improve next.

Testing Vocabulary

A prototype is an early version of a design built so the team can learn from it.

A mockup is a simple model or rough build used to check shape, fit, or motion.

A testable design is a design that can be tried safely and measured in some way.

A hypothesis is a prediction your team can test.

A variable is one thing that can change, such as motor power, gap size, or launch angle.

A control is something your team keeps the same during a test so the results are easier to understand.

A measurement is a number or clear observation your team records.

Evidence is information from testing, not just an opinion.

A trial is one attempt or test run.

An iteration is a new version made after learning from a test.

Alignment means parts are lined up so the ball moves through the shooter smoothly.

The ball path is the route the ball follows through the shooter.

The launch point is where the ball leaves the shooter.

A ramp is a sloped surface that guides the ball into or out of the wheel.

A hood is a curved or angled guide that holds the ball near the flywheel as it accelerates.

A guide rail is a side piece that keeps the ball moving in the intended path.

A mounting point is where a motor, bracket, ramp, or guide attaches to the robot.

An adjustment is a planned change your team can make, such as moving a guide rail or changing motor power.

A failure point is a place where the design is likely to loosen, jam, bend, slip, or break.

What Your Shooter Must Do

Your shooter must:

  • hold the motor firmly,
  • hold the wheel securely,
  • guide the ball into the wheel,
  • squeeze the ball enough to grip it,
  • let the ball leave in a predictable direction,
  • and allow the team to adjust something, such as motor power, gap size, or angle.

If the shooter cannot be adjusted, it is harder to improve from test evidence.

Plan Before You Build

Before building, identify:

  • where the motor will mount,
  • where the wheel will spin,
  • where the ball enters,
  • where the ball exits,
  • the planned launch angle,
  • the planned compression gap,
  • how the team will keep hands away from the spinning wheel,
  • and how the team will stop the motor safely.

First-Build Checklist

  • Motor is mounted firmly.
  • Wheel is attached securely.
  • Wheel spins freely without rubbing.
  • Ball path is clear.
  • Compression gap is measured or estimated.
  • Wires are away from the spinning wheel.
  • Team can test with the robot or shooter held safely.
  • Team has a plan to stop the motor.

Write a Testable Hypothesis

A useful hypothesis names the change and the result you expect.

Examples:

  • If we decrease the gap between the wheel and the ramp, then the ball will launch faster, but the motor may slow down more.
  • If we increase motor power, then the ball will travel farther, but it may hit the goal harder and bounce out.
  • If we use a softer wheel, then the ball may grip better, but the launch speed may be less consistent.

Safety Warnings

Spinning wheels can pinch fingers.

Keep hair, strings, hoodie cords, and wires away from the flywheel.

Do not hold the ball in the wheel by hand while the motor is running.

Stop the OpMode before adjusting the shooter.

Reflection
