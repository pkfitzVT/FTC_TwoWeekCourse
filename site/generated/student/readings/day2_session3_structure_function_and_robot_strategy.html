Structure, Function, and Robot Strategy

A Robot Is a Strategy Made Physical

A robot is not just a collection of parts.

A robot is a strategy made physical.

That means the shape, frame, mechanisms, controls, and code should all support what the team is trying to accomplish in the game.

Before your team chooses a chassis shape, shooter location, hopper design, trigger, or controller layout, ask:

What job does this part need to do?

Then ask:

What strategy does this design support?

---

Structure and Function in Nature

In nature, structures usually match functions.

A structure is how something is built or shaped.

A function is what that structure helps it do.

Examples:

  • A bird's beak shape helps it eat certain foods.
  • A fish's body shape helps it move through water.
  • Hollow bones can be strong while staying light.
  • Shells protect soft bodies from damage.
  • Plant stems support leaves so they can reach light.
  • Roots spread through soil to hold the plant and collect water.

These forms are not random. They help living things survive, move, eat, grow, or protect themselves.

The same idea matters in engineering.

---

Structure and Function in Human Design

Bridges, buildings, tools, and machines also connect structure to function.

A bridge is shaped to carry loads across a gap. A building frame is shaped to resist gravity, wind, and movement. A handle is shaped so a human hand can grip it. A bicycle frame is shaped to support weight while staying light enough to move.

Good design is not only about making something strong. It is about making it strong enough for the right job, light enough for the right movement, and simple enough to build, use, and repair.

That is why engineers ask:

  • What forces will this structure feel?
  • What must this part support, guide, push, launch, or protect?
  • What must stay still?
  • What must move?
  • What does this choice make easier?
  • What does this choice make harder?

---

Robot Form Should Follow Game Strategy

In this course, your robot is designed for a modified robotics game. Your team may score artifacts, move artifacts, work with a human operator, avoid penalties, run autonomous code, and park during endgame.

Different strategies lead to different robot forms.

Strategy Need Possible Design Implication
Score from one known position Chassis and shooter should make lining up repeatable.
Complete multiple TeleOp scoring loops Robot should drive reliably, reload quickly, and avoid jams.
Push artifacts toward the human operator Front shape, bumper area, and driver visibility matter.
Release artifacts from a sleeve Hopper path, trigger placement, and timing matter.
Park fully during endgame Chassis size, turning control, and driver routine matter.
Coordinate so both teams park Robot width, field awareness, and safe driving matter.
Attempt autonomous scoring Starting position, code consistency, and shooter alignment matter.

There is no perfect robot shape. There is only a shape that fits the job better or worse.

---

One Choice Changes the Whole Robot

Robot parts affect each other.

Choosing a wider chassis may create more room for a hopper, but it may make parking or turning harder. Moving the shooter may improve aiming, but it may block the battery or make the robot top-heavy. Adding a mechanism may increase scoring, but it may also create more places to jam, loosen, or fail.

This is called a design tradeoff.

A tradeoff is not automatically bad. It means your team must decide what matters most.

Before making a major design choice, ask:

  • What function does this structure support?
  • What game strategy does this choice help?
  • What other part of the robot will this affect?
  • What will this make harder for the driver, builder, programmer, or pit crew?
  • What evidence would tell us whether this choice is working?

---

Connect This to the Team Strategy Guide

Your Team Strategy Guide should help your team decide what the robot needs to do before you decide what the robot should look like.

Use this order:

1. Understand the modified game. 2. Choose team norms for how you will work together. 3. Choose a first game strategy. 4. Estimate how the strategy might score. 5. Decide what robot structures and mechanisms would support that strategy. 6. Build, test, collect evidence, and revise.

If your strategy changes, your design may need to change.

If your design cannot support your strategy, your strategy may need to change.

Good teams keep those two ideas connected.

What is one part of our robot that should be shaped by our scoring strategy? 4. What design choice might create a tradeoff for our team? 5. Before we build, what evidence or observation would help us choose a better structure?
