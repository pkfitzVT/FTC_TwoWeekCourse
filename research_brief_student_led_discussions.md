# Research Brief: Student-Led Discussions

## FTC YES Course Design

## Course Design Feature

Each two-hour course session includes at least one Student-Led Discussion (SLD). Most SLDs serve one of two purposes:

- **Decision SLD:** Students discuss factors that should influence a team or class decision before acting.
- **Synthesis SLD:** Students connect recent information, readings, videos, demonstrations, or experiences into a clearer internal understanding.

Teachers track discussion moves over time, including use of background documents, personal anecdotes, logical assertions, questions, and building on another student's idea.

## Problem This Process Addresses

Robotics courses can become dominated by action: build, drive, code, fix, repeat. Without structured time for discussion, students may act before reasoning through trade-offs, and a few confident voices may shape decisions without the group understanding or owning them.

SLDs are designed to slow down key moments just enough for students to use evidence, ask questions, hear other perspectives, and turn experience into shared understanding.

## Description of the Problem

In robotics teams, students often face decisions with no single correct answer. For example:

- Should we protect electronics or keep them accessible?
- Should we build a simple reliable chassis or leave room for future mechanisms?
- Should we use D-pad drive, joystick drive, or joystick with speed control?
- Should scarce materials go to the team that asks first or the team with the clearest test plan?
- What do we owe teams that lend us equipment?

If these questions are handled informally, decisions may be shaped by speed, confidence, prior experience, or social status rather than careful reasoning. Some students may never practice explaining technical ideas, asking useful questions, or challenging a plan constructively. This weakens both engineering learning and team culture.

## Research Summary

Research on small-group STEM discussion supports the value of structured talk when students are learning complex science and engineering ideas. Bennett et al. (2010) reviewed research on small-group discussions in high school science and found that discussions are more productive when students receive explicit support, group-work training, and cues for how to participate. The same review noted that students often struggle to form coherent arguments without this structure.

Recent STEM discussion research also emphasizes that small-group discussions can help students ask questions, build explanations, use evidence, consider multiple perspectives, and revise their thinking (Firetto et al., 2023). These are the same practices students need when making engineering decisions under constraints.

Discussion research also highlights an equity concern. Students do not all experience discussion spaces the same way. Clarke et al. (2016) found that students may differ not only in how often they participate, but in whether they perceive themselves as having agency to participate. This matters in a robotics course because the students who speak most readily may not be the only students with useful ideas.

Equity research in STEM further supports tracking participation patterns. Reinholz et al. (2024) describe how participation data can help educators notice inequitable patterns and adjust instruction. Their work builds on Complex Instruction, which emphasizes group-worthy tasks, multiple-ability framing, roles, and public recognition of student competence.

## Why This Course Design Is a Good Response

SLDs make reasoning visible before and after technical work. They help students practice the kinds of moves that strong engineering teams need:

- citing evidence,
- asking clarifying questions,
- naming trade-offs,
- connecting experience to design decisions,
- challenging assumptions,
- building on another person's thinking,
- and deciding what evidence should be collected next.

The teacher tracking system is especially useful because it tracks the **type** of contribution, not just the amount of talking. This reduces the risk that the loudest or most confident students are automatically treated as the strongest contributors.

## Expected Student Gains

Students should improve in:

- evidence-based reasoning,
- listening and responding to peers,
- asking useful questions,
- explaining trade-offs,
- making decisions with more shared ownership,
- connecting robotics to FIRST values,
- internalizing new information,
- and participating in a more equitable team culture.

## Examples of Desired Outcomes

During a borrowed-materials SLD, students might say:

- "The equipment is borrowed from teams that need it back, so if we break something we need to report it right away."
- "This reminds me of borrowing sports equipment. If people do not return it, the next team loses access."
- "If teams share materials, trust becomes part of the system."
- "What should happen if two teams need the same part at the same time?"
- "I want to build on what Jordan said about trust. It is not just about one part; it affects whether teams will lend to us again."

Over time, success would look like:

- more students participating,
- more students asking questions,
- more students citing handouts, videos, rules, or test data,
- less dependence on teacher prompting,
- and portfolio entries that show clearer reasoning before decisions.

## Potential Obstacles

Likely challenges include:

- students treating discussion as separate from robot work,
- dominant voices taking over,
- quiet students avoiding public risk,
- students sharing opinions without evidence,
- students speaking to the teacher instead of each other,
- and limited session time.

## Implementation Strategies

Use a consistent SLD routine:

1. Provide a short reference reading, video, demonstration, or experience.
2. Give students one clear discussion question.
3. Remind students of discussion moves.
4. Track group-level participation patterns.
5. End with a decision, synthesis statement, or portfolio prompt.

Useful discussion moves:

- "The handout says..."
- "A real example is..."
- "My claim is..."
- "One question I have is..."
- "I want to build on..."
- "A trade-off is..."
- "The evidence we should collect is..."

## Connection to Best Practices in Education

SLDs connect to:

- collaborative learning,
- discussion-based learning,
- project-based learning,
- student agency,
- formative assessment,
- metacognition,
- culturally responsive teaching,
- evidence-based reasoning,
- and engineering design practice.

They also support the shift from teacher-centered explanation to student sensemaking. The teacher still designs the conditions, but students carry more of the thinking.

## Notes on Evidence Quality

The strongest research base for this brief comes from STEM discussion, science argumentation, and equitable participation research. Some research involves high school science classrooms, while other studies involve undergraduate STEM contexts. The findings are still relevant because the YES robotics course relies on similar practices: collaborative reasoning, evidence use, student agency, and discussion of technical problems.
