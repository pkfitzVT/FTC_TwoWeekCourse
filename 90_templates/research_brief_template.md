# Research Brief Template

## FTC YES Course Design

Use this template for short research briefs that support the design of the FTC YES robotics course. Each brief should explain a course-design problem, summarize relevant research, connect the research to a specific course structure, and identify the evidence students or teachers will collect.

## Research Selection Criteria

Research briefs for this course should prioritize:

- peer-reviewed publications when available,
- recent research over older research when quality is similar,
- highly cited research when it is relevant,
- high school-aged students when available,
- U.S.-based educational contexts when relevant,
- STEM, engineering design, robotics, project-based learning, student discussion, collaboration, equity, student agency, and assessment research,
- studies connected to FIRST, FTC, VEX, or competitive educational robotics when available.

When high school or robotics-specific research is limited, briefs may draw from related K-12 STEM, undergraduate STEM, engineering education, science education, or collaborative learning research. In those cases, the brief should explain the limitation and why the research is still useful for this course.

# Brief Title

## Course Design Feature

Name the practice, structure, or document the research supports.

Examples:

- Student-led discussions
- Design menu cards
- Must / Should / Could challenge tiers
- Living engineering portfolio
- Role rotation by subsystem
- Materials proposal process
- Borrowed equipment stewardship norms

## Problem This Process Addresses

State the problem in one or two clear sentences.

Example:

Unstructured robotics teams can allow a few confident or experienced students to dominate technical tasks and decision-making while other students become observers or note-takers.

## Description of the Problem

Describe how the problem appears in classrooms, robotics teams, or student groups.

Consider:

- Who is most affected?
- What learning opportunities are lost?
- How does the problem show up in behavior?
- Why might teachers or mentors miss it?
- Why does it matter for equity, learning, safety, or assessment?

## Research Summary

Summarize the most relevant research.

Include:

- what kind of research was conducted,
- who participated,
- the main findings,
- how strongly the research applies to this course,
- any important limitations.

Use embedded citations in APA style.

## Why This Course Design Is a Good Response

Explain why the proposed course feature addresses the problem.

Consider:

- How does the feature change student behavior?
- How does it make thinking, participation, or evidence visible?
- How does it support students with different experience levels?
- How does it help teachers or mentors intervene earlier?

## Expected Student Gains

List the learning or development gains expected from using this process.

Possible gains:

- more equitable participation,
- better use of evidence,
- clearer engineering decisions,
- stronger student agency,
- improved technical confidence,
- stronger communication,
- better documentation,
- stronger teamwork,
- better reflection,
- improved connection to BHS Graduate Expectations.

## Examples of Desired Outcomes

Describe what success would look like in the course.

Examples:

- Students cite a handout or test result during an SLD.
- A team records multiple design options before choosing one.
- Discussion tracking shows more students asking questions over time.
- Portfolio entries show trade-offs, test data, and revisions.
- Students can explain what they gained and gave up in a design decision.

## Potential Obstacles

Name likely implementation challenges.

Examples:

- dominant students resist slowing down,
- quieter students avoid public participation,
- students treat the process as paperwork,
- teachers do not have time to track everything,
- students confuse opinions with evidence,
- teams rush to build before discussing trade-offs.

## Implementation Strategies

Name specific ways to reduce those obstacles.

Examples:

- use sentence starters,
- assign rotating roles,
- use short decision tables,
- track group-level discussion moves,
- require portfolio evidence,
- use a timer for proposal rounds,
- use teacher/mentor check-ins,
- model a completed example.
