# Challenge by Choice: Robotics Challenge Pathways

In this robotics course, every team is building, testing, documenting, and presenting a working robot. Some students may also choose a **Challenge by Choice** pathway.

These challenges are not extra busywork, and they are not permanent jobs. They are invitations to go deeper in a way that helps your team.

## Our Course Culture

This course is not mainly about winning a robotics competition. It is about building something together.

Robotics works best when students encourage each other to take risks, try unfamiliar tools, ask questions, and learn from mistakes. Nobody is expected to already know how to build, code, design, test, or present. The goal is to participate, contribute, and grow.

Challenge by Choice does not mean you are locked into one role. You are not "the CAD person," "the coder," or "the builder" forever. You may try different kinds of work during the course. You might build one day, test code another day, help with the hopper, collect physics data, or explain the team's design to others.

A strong team makes room for everyone to participate. More experienced students should not take over the project. They should help invite others into the work. Less experienced students should not be pushed to the side. The team succeeds when people teach each other, invite each other in, and build confidence together.

The point of this experience is to make new friendships, become more curious, learn how things work, and see how engineering can help people solve real problems in the world.

## Low Floor, High Ceiling

This course is designed with a low floor and a high ceiling.

**Low floor** means everyone can enter the work. You can help by tightening bolts, testing the robot, watching for jams, taking photos, organizing parts, asking good questions, or helping your team explain what happened.

**High ceiling** means there is always room to go deeper. You can design CAD parts, organize Java code, analyze projectile motion, use statistics, prepare a stronger presentation, or connect the robot to future courses and careers.

Both kinds of work matter. A robot does not get built by one expert. It gets built by a team.

## Team Norms

During this course, we will try to:

- invite people into the work
- explain what we are doing
- let others try tools and code
- ask before taking over
- treat mistakes as part of engineering
- use evidence from testing
- celebrate small improvements
- help the team, not just our own idea
- value curiosity more than already knowing the answer

## Why Challenge by Choice?

Robotics connects many subjects at once:

- Geometry helps us design parts.
- Physics helps us understand motion.
- Programming helps us control mechanisms.
- Statistics helps us use test data.
- Writing and speaking help us explain our work.
- Teamwork helps us solve problems faster.

You may choose a challenge because you already have experience in an area, or because you want to try something new. You may also try part of a challenge, work with a partner, switch later, or bring what you learn back to your team.

The goal is not to separate students by skill level. The goal is to make sure everyone has a meaningful way to participate and that students who want more depth have a path to keep growing.

## Challenge Pathways

| Challenge Pathway | Good Fit For Students Interested In | Team Contribution |
|---|---|---|
| CAD / Fabrication Challenge | Geometry, design, engineering, architecture, art, laser cutting | Design an adjustable shooter mount or other custom part |
| Software Engineering Challenge | Java, AP Computer Science A, AI coding tools, app development | Organize robot code into methods or classes |
| Physics / Data Challenge | AP Physics, AP Calculus, AP Statistics, data science | Analyze projectile motion and recommend shooter settings |
| Reliability / Inspection Challenge | Engineering, motorsports, aviation, safety systems | Help the robot pass inspection and become more reliable |
| Presentation / Story Challenge | Public speaking, business, leadership, design communication | Help tell the team's engineering story in 4 minutes |

## How to Choose a Challenge

Choose a challenge that matches one of these statements:

- "I want to design a physical part."
- "I want to work with code."
- "I want to use math or physics."
- "I want to collect and analyze data."
- "I want to help the robot become more reliable."
- "I want to help my team explain what we built."
- "I want to try something difficult and learn as I go."

You may work alone, with a partner, or with a small group, depending on the challenge and your team's needs. You should still stay connected to the main team build.

## Expectations

If you choose a challenge, you should produce something useful for your team.

| Challenge | Possible Deliverable |
|---|---|
| CAD / Fabrication | Sketch, CAD model, DXF/SVG file, laser-cut prototype, mounting pattern |
| Software Engineering | Cleaner TeleOp code, helper methods, shooter/trigger methods, custom class |
| Physics / Data | Video analysis, Desmos graph, regression model, launch angle recommendation |
| Reliability / Inspection | Inspection checklist, repair list, safety fixes, readiness report |
| Presentation / Story | 4-minute script, visuals, team story, award evidence |

Your work should help the team build, test, improve, or explain the robot. Challenge work should come back to the team instead of pulling one person away from everyone else.

## Daily Challenge Check-In

At the end of each work session, be ready to answer:

1. What did I work on today?
2. What did I learn?
3. How does this help the robot or the team?
4. What does the team need to know?
5. Who else can I invite into this work?
6. What is my next step?

## Team Rule

Challenge work should not isolate you from the team.

Your job is to make a useful temporary contribution **for your team**, not separate from your team.

Bring your learning back. Explain what you found. Help others understand. Invite someone else to try part of the work. Ask for help when you need it.

## Using AI Tools

You may use AI tools such as ChatGPT, Claude, GitHub Copilot, or other approved tools to help you:

- understand vocabulary
- plan code
- debug errors
- explain math
- brainstorm designs
- improve writing
- prepare presentations

But AI does not replace your thinking or your participation.

AI should help more students enter the work, not help one student take over. If AI helps you understand code, CAD, physics, or presentation writing, use that knowledge to explain ideas to your teammates and invite them into the process.

You are responsible for testing ideas, checking whether the answer makes sense, and explaining your work to the team.

## Final Reflection

At the end of the course, you should be able to explain:

- What challenge pathway you tried
- Why you chose it
- What you created or contributed
- What was difficult
- How you used feedback or testing
- How your work helped the team
- Who you invited into the work
- What course or career this challenge connects to
- What you might want to learn next
