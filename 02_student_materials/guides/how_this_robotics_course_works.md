# How This Robotics Course Works

Design, Build, Program, Test, Improve, and Document

In this course, your team will design, build, program, test, and improve a robot for a modified FTC-style challenge.

At the beginning, your teachers will provide more structure. As your team gains experience, you will take more ownership of your decisions, your robot, and your work process.

The goal is not only to build a robot. The goal is to learn how engineering teams work: ask questions, use evidence, make decisions, test ideas, learn from mistakes, and improve.

## What Your Team Will Build

Your team will build and connect several major robot subsystems.

| Subsystem | Main Job |
|---|---|
| Chassis / drivetrain | Move, turn, park, and share space with other robots |
| Flywheel shooter | Launch balls consistently |
| Hopper | Hold and feed balls |
| Trigger / servo | Release balls at the right time |

Later, your team will integrate these systems so the robot can complete a full scoring cycle. If time permits, autonomous programming adds another layer.

## The Repeated Engineering Cycle

You will use the same basic cycle many times.

```text
Game Goals
-> Robot Behavior Targets
-> Design SLD
-> Build
-> Program / Configure
-> Test
-> Operations Role SLD
-> Integrated Performance Test
-> Postmortem / Tuning SLD
-> Revise or Accept
-> Document
```

You will repeat this pattern for the chassis, flywheel, hopper, trigger, and whole-robot integration.

A subsystem is not just a physical mechanism. It includes hardware, software or controls, human operation, testing evidence, and documentation.

## Student-Led Discussions

Student-Led Discussion, or SLD, is how your team turns individual ideas into shared decisions.

SLD is the process.

The work product is the result.

Strong teams:

- ask questions
- use evidence
- listen
- invite different voices
- name tradeoffs
- disagree respectfully
- make a decision
- document the decision

| Type of SLD | When It Happens | What It Produces |
|---|---|---|
| Identity / Norms SLD | Early in the course | Team norms and identity |
| Strategy / Behavior SLD | Before major robot decisions | Game strategy and robot behavior targets |
| Design SLD | Before building or major revisions | Subsystem design plan |
| Operations Role SLD | Before performance testing | Primary roles, backups, and practice plan |
| Postmortem / Tuning SLD | After testing | Diagnosis, next change, and retest plan |

Learn more in the [Student-Led Discussion Reference Guide](student_led_discussion_reference_guide.md).

## Micro-Decisions During Work Time

Not every team decision needs a formal SLD. During work time, your team will make many small decisions.

Examples:

- Who is working on the wiring?
- Which bracket fits best?
- Should we test-fit this before tightening it?
- Is this wire route safe?
- Is this part blocking another subsystem?
- Should we record this change?

These small decisions matter. If a choice changes the design, code, safety, or strategy, record it in your engineering portfolio.

## Test, Diagnose, and Improve

Robot testing should lead to evidence-based revision. When something does not work, try to classify the issue before changing the robot.

| Category | Example |
|---|---|
| Human Operation | Driver overcorrects or operator timing is inconsistent |
| Mechanical System | Loose wheel, jam, rubbing part, weak mount |
| Software / Controls | Speed too high, button mapping confusing, servo range wrong |
| Strategy / Communication | Unclear roles, no recovery plan, poor communication |

A postmortem is not about blame. It is about understanding what happened so the team can improve.

Use the [Reusable Performance Testing Postmortem SLD](../sld_prompts/reusable_performance_testing_postmortem_sld.md) after important tests or practice runs.

## Stop Chasing Your Tail

A robot can always be improved. Your team must decide when a subsystem is reliable enough to use.

Do not change everything after one bad run.

Change one or two things.

Run several trials.

Record what happened.

Look for patterns.

Stop tuning when the subsystem works inside an acceptable range.

For example, if your team changes launch angle, motor power, ball position, and trigger timing after every shot, you will not know which change caused the result.

Learn more in the [Subsystem Design, Operation, Testing, and Tuning Cycle Guide](subsystem_design_operation_testing_cycle_guide.md).

## Challenge by Choice

Many tasks use a Must / Should / Could structure.

| Level | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Must | Everyone completes this core task |
| Should | Most teams should try this next step |
| Could | Choose this challenge if your team is ready |

Challenge by Choice does not mean avoiding hard work. It means choosing the right next challenge and taking thoughtful risks.

## Advanced Pathways

Some students or teams may choose extensions that connect robotics to other interests and classes.

| Pathway | Possible Extension |
|---|---|
| Math | Video analysis, projectile motion, regression, modeling |
| Physics / Science | Launch angle, velocity, force, energy, motion |
| Statistics | Scoring probability, variability, confidence, data visualization |
| Programming | Reusable classes, methods, object-oriented design, autonomous code |

These pathways are opportunities to connect robotics to your interests and other classes.

## Documentation Happens the Whole Time

Your engineering portfolio is not something you write at the end. It is the record of your team's decisions, tests, revisions, and growth.

## Engineering Portfolio Evidence

- [ ] team norms and strategy
- [ ] robot behavior targets
- [ ] design decisions
- [ ] sketches, photos, or diagrams
- [ ] parts and materials
- [ ] configuration names
- [ ] code snippets or control mapping
- [ ] test data
- [ ] operator/driver notes
- [ ] postmortem diagnosis
- [ ] revisions
- [ ] retest results
- [ ] acceptable performance range or next step

Your first version will not be perfect. That is normal. Engineering is a cycle: make a plan, build a version, test it, learn from the result, and improve. As your team practices this cycle, your teachers will step back and your team will take more ownership.
