# Principles of Robot Assembly  
## Student Guide for FTC Robot Building

**Recommended course placement:** Day 3, when chassis assembly begins.  
This guide works well immediately before students begin building the drivetrain/chassis because it gives them habits for counting holes, aligning parts, using fasteners correctly, and avoiding damage to shared robot materials.

---

## Purpose

Building a robot is not just about attaching parts. Good assembly is about thinking ahead, working carefully, and making choices that make the robot stronger, easier to fix, and easier for your team to improve.

The goal is not to build fast.

The goal is to build well.

---

## 1. Brackets Turn Parts Into Structures

Beams, channels, plates, and extrusion do not become a robot frame by themselves. **Brackets** are the parts that connect loose pieces and help turn them into a structure.

Brackets are used to:

- join rails, channels, plates, or extrusion
- hold parts at right angles
- create corners
- support loads
- keep the chassis square
- make the robot easier to repair or modify

A bracket is not just a random connector. It is a structural choice.

### REV Plastic Brackets Need Care

REV plastic brackets are useful, light, and easy to work with. They can also break if screws are overtightened.

> Tight means secure. Tight does not mean crushed.

Tighten plastic brackets carefully. Stop if the bracket starts bending, cracking, or deforming.

This connects to a larger assembly rule: tighten parts with the intention to take them apart later. Soft fasteners and plastic parts can be damaged if you use too much force.

### Start Screws Before Sliding Brackets Into Position

When using REV brackets, it is often easier to put screws into the bracket first, before sliding the bracket into a channel or moving it into position.

This helps because:

- the bracket can slide into and out of position more easily
- the screws are easier to keep track of
- the team is less likely to drop or lose screws
- it saves time and energy
- it avoids taking apart larger sections just to add a screw later

Rule of thumb:

> If the bracket will slide into a slot or channel, start the screws first.

### Inside and Outside Corner Brackets

Inside and outside corner brackets can both support a structure, but they support it differently.

**Inside corner brackets**:

- sit inside the corner
- help hold parts at a right angle
- can be protected from impacts
- may be harder to reach after the robot is assembled

**Outside corner brackets**:

- sit outside the corner
- are often easier to inspect and tighten
- can make repairs easier
- may take up outside space or be more exposed to impacts

The best choice depends on access, strength, and what else needs to fit nearby.

Before choosing a bracket location, ask:

> Will we be able to reach this bracket later?

### Add Extra Slot Hardware Only When It Has a Purpose

If you know your team will later add something to a REV channel slot, you can add extra screws or slot hardware early, before the structure is closed up.

> A screw added early can save ten minutes later.

This does not mean adding random loose hardware everywhere. Extra screws should be intentional. They should not interfere with moving parts, wheels, belts, chains, wires, or future mechanisms.

### Brackets Do Not Need Every Hole Filled

Brackets do not need screws in every hole.

Usually, two well-placed screws are enough to lock a bracket in place. One screw is usually not enough because the bracket can rotate.

One screw can be acceptable temporarily for a proof of concept, mockup, or quick alignment check.

> One screw can help you test an idea. Two screws make it a structure.

This is the same idea as the later rule about fasteners: one fastener can allow movement, but two fasteners usually lock things in place.

## 2. When in Doubt, Make It Symmetrical

Symmetry usually makes a robot stronger, straighter, and easier to understand.

If one side of the robot has a support, spacer, bracket, or screw pattern, the other side should often match it.

Before you attach a part, ask:

> Should this be the same on both sides?

Symmetry helps with:

- Straight driving
- Balanced weight
- Easier repairs
- Cleaner design
- Easier communication with teammates

A robot does not always have to be perfectly symmetrical, but if you do not have a clear reason to make it different, symmetry is usually the safer choice.

---

## 3. Count Holes Twice

Many FTC parts use repeating hole patterns. A motor, bracket, beam, or plate may look correct even when it is one hole off.

Before tightening anything, count carefully.

> Moving a motor later can take 10-20 minutes.  
> Counting holes now takes 10-20 seconds.

Good builders check alignment before tightening.

Helpful habits:

- Count from the same end on both parts.
- Compare the left and right sides.
- Ask a teammate to check your count.
- Look at the part from above, not just from the side.

---

## 4. Start Finger Tight

When assembling several screws and nuts, do not fully tighten the first one right away.

Instead:

1. Place all the screws.
2. Add the nuts finger tight.
3. Check that everything lines up.
4. Then tighten gradually.

This gives the parts a little room to shift into position.

If you tighten one screw fully before the others are placed, the part may twist, bind, or no longer line up with the remaining holes.

---

## 5. One Nut Allows Movement. Two Nuts Lock Things in Place.

A single screw and nut can act like a pivot point. The part may still rotate around that connection.

Two fasteners usually lock a part in place because the part can no longer spin freely.

Think of it this way:

> One fastener holds a part.  
> Two fasteners control the part.

When you want something fixed in place, use two fasteners whenever possible.

---

## 6. Place Two Fasteners Opposite Each Other When Possible

If a part is held by two screws, try to place them far apart or across from each other.

This gives the connection more strength and reduces twisting.

Weak pattern:

```text
[ screw ][ screw ]
```

Stronger pattern:

```text
[ screw ]     [ screw ]
```

Even better, when the part allows it, place fasteners diagonally or opposite each other.

This is especially important for:

- Motor mounts
- Structural brackets
- Wheel supports
- Arm supports
- Heavy mechanisms

---

## 7. Do Not Fight Gravity

Gravity can help you or fight you.

Whenever possible, place the part flat on the table and drop screws down from above.

Then add nuts underneath or from the easiest side.

A good question to ask is:

> Can I turn this part so gravity helps hold things in place?

It is much harder to assemble a part while holding it sideways in the air, balancing a nut, and trying to line up a screw.

Use the table. Rotate the robot. Reposition the part.

Good builders change the setup so the work becomes easier.

---

## 8. Assemble Over a Tablecloth, Tray, or Aluminum Sheet

Dropped nuts, screws, washers, spacers, and set screws are part of robotics. They happen to everyone.

But you can make them easier to find.

Whenever possible, assemble over:

- A tablecloth
- A tray
- A shallow box lid
- A light-colored towel
- A sheet of aluminum or metal

Small parts are much easier to find on a clean surface than on the floor.

This matters because some parts are difficult to replace during class.

---

## 9. Dropped Nuts Happen

Do not panic when you drop a nut, screw, or washer.

Stop moving for a second. Look carefully. Ask nearby teammates not to step on the area.

If the part is important, find it before continuing.

Especially watch for:

- Set screws
- Shaft collars
- Small spacers
- Specialty nuts
- Washers
- Bearing parts

A lost normal nut is annoying.

A lost set screw can stop an entire mechanism from working.

---

## 10. Check That Every Collar Has a Set Screw

Shaft collars are small but critical. They keep shafts, wheels, gears, and other parts from sliding out of place.

Most collars use a tiny set screw.

Before using a collar, check:

- Is the set screw actually there?
- Is it threaded into the collar?
- Is it loose enough to slide onto the shaft?
- Is it tightened once the collar is in the right place?

Set screws are very difficult to replace when lost.

Treat them like important equipment.

---

## 11. Tighten With the Intention to Take It Apart Later

FTC robots change constantly. A part you install today may need to move tomorrow.

Do not overtighten fasteners.

Tighten parts enough that they are secure, but remember:

> You, or someone else, may need to take this apart later.

Fasteners are soft. Tools can strip them. Parts can be damaged.

You can absolutely break a screw, strip a nut, or ruin a piece of material.

Good builders tighten carefully.

---

## 12. Use the Right Amount of Force

If something is not working, do not just push harder.

Stop and ask:

- Is the screw straight?
- Is the nut cross-threaded?
- Are the holes aligned?
- Is the part upside down?
- Am I using the correct tool?
- Is this the correct fastener?

Forcing parts usually creates bigger problems.

A good rule:

> If it feels wrong, stop and check.

---

## 13. Use T-Slot Nuts on the Outside When Possible

T-slot nuts can be useful, but they are only useful where they can actually slide into place and be adjusted.

Use them on the outside of extrusion whenever possible.

They usually do not work well once they are trapped inside extrusion slots or blocked by other parts.

Before choosing a T-slot nut, ask:

> Will I still be able to reach, move, or remove this later?

If not, choose a different connection method.

---

## 14. Make the Assembly Easy to Inspect

A good robot is not just strong. It is understandable.

Try to build so someone else can look at your work and answer:

- What is this part doing?
- How is it attached?
- Can we reach the screws?
- Can we tighten it later?
- Can we remove it without taking apart half the robot?

Hidden screws and trapped nuts make repairs harder.

Design for access.

---

## 15. Ask for Help Before You Get Stuck Too Long

Frustration is normal. Robotics is full of small alignment problems.

If you feel yourself getting frustrated:

1. Put the tool down.
2. Take a short break.
3. Ask someone to look at the problem with you.
4. Explain what you are trying to do.

Sometimes another person can see the issue quickly.

Asking for help is not failure. It is part of engineering.

---

## 16. Get Someone Else to Start You If Needed

Sometimes the hardest part is getting the first screw, bracket, or nut started.

It is okay to ask:

> Can someone help me get this started?

Once the first connection is aligned, the rest of the assembly is often much easier.

Teams build better robots when students help each other through stuck points.

---

## 17. Build So the Next Team Can Use the Parts

The robot parts belong to the program, not just to one team.

Treat every beam, plate, screw, nut, collar, motor, and bearing like another student will need it later.

That means:

- Do not strip screws.
- Do not overtighten.
- Do not bend parts unless instructed.
- Do not force holes to fit.
- Do not lose specialty hardware.
- Do not damage reusable materials.

A good builder leaves parts usable for the next person.

# Assembly Checklist

Before tightening a part fully, check:

```text
[ ] Are the holes counted correctly?
[ ] Is the part symmetrical if it should be?
[ ] Are brackets placed where they support the structure?
[ ] Are plastic brackets tightened carefully, not crushed?
[ ] Are screws started before sliding brackets into channel slots?
[ ] Are inside/outside brackets still reachable later?
[ ] Did we add intentional extra slot hardware if we know we will need it later?
[ ] Are we using two screws for permanent bracket connections whenever possible?
[ ] Are all screws started finger tight?
[ ] Are there at least two fasteners if the part must not rotate?
[ ] Are the fasteners spaced far enough apart?
[ ] Can I reach these screws later?
[ ] Am I using gravity to help me?
[ ] Am I working over a surface where dropped parts can be found?
[ ] Are collars checked for set screws?
[ ] Am I tightening carefully, not forcing anything?
[ ] Would another team be able to take this apart later?
```

---

# The Big Idea

Good robot assembly is careful, patient, and thoughtful.

The best builders are not the people who tighten everything the fastest. The best builders are the people who:

- Check alignment
- Think ahead
- Protect the parts
- Help teammates
- Build systems that can be repaired
- Stay calm when something does not work

Build carefully now so the robot works better later.
