# Session 7 Reading: Chassis Build Checklist

## From Design to Robot

Day 4 is where your team starts turning a chassis design into a physical robot. A sketch is useful, but the real robot has to fit the parts you have, stay together, leave room for wiring, and be safe to test.

Before you build, slow down enough to check your plan. A few careful minutes now can prevent loose parts, blocked wires, or a robot that is hard to repair later.

## Build Vocabulary

A **chassis** is the base of the robot. It holds the drivetrain, electronics, battery, and future mechanisms.

A **drivetrain** is the part of the robot that makes it move across the floor. It usually includes motors, wheels, axles, and the frame pieces that support them.

A **frame** is the main structure of the chassis. It gives the robot its shape and supports the parts attached to it.

A **rail** is a long frame piece. Teams often use rails to make the sides or front/back of a chassis.

A **bracket** is a connector piece that joins parts together, often at a corner or angle.

A **fastener** is hardware used to hold parts together. Screws, bolts, and nuts are fasteners.

A **nut** is a small threaded piece that tightens onto a bolt or screw.

A **bolt** is a threaded fastener that passes through parts and is usually tightened with a nut.

A **washer** is a flat ring that spreads force under a bolt or nut. It can help protect parts and reduce loosening.

A **standoff** is a spacer that holds two parts a fixed distance apart.

A **motor mount** is a part or bracket that holds a motor firmly to the frame.

A **wheel** is the round part that touches the floor and moves the robot.

A **hub** is the center part that connects a wheel to an axle or motor shaft.

An **axle** is a rod that a wheel spins on or with.

A **bearing** is a part that helps a shaft or axle spin smoothly with less rubbing.

**Clearance** is the space between the robot and the floor or between two robot parts.

The **center of gravity** is the point where the robot's weight is balanced. A high or uneven center of gravity can make a robot less stable.

**Access** means being able to reach a part for repair, charging, wiring, or adjustment.

A **constraint** is a limit your team must work within, such as robot size, time, tools, parts, or safety.

A **tradeoff** is what you give up when you choose one design over another. A larger chassis may leave more space, but it may be harder to turn in tight areas.

## Tool Vocabulary

A **hex key** or **Allen wrench** is an L-shaped tool used for screws with hex-shaped holes.

A **wrench** grips nuts or bolts so you can tighten or loosen them.

A **screwdriver** turns screws. Use the correct size so the screw head does not strip.

A **nut driver** works like a screwdriver but fits over a nut.

**Pliers** grip, bend, or hold small parts. They are not a replacement for every wrench.

A **wire** carries electrical power or signals between robot parts.

A **zip tie** is a plastic strap used to hold wires neatly and away from moving parts.

A **battery** stores the electrical energy that powers the robot.

A **charger** safely recharges the robot battery. Use the charger only as instructed.

## Before You Build

- Review your chassis sketch and decision notes.
- Identify the front, back, left side, and right side of the robot.
- Find the frame pieces, brackets, fasteners, motors, wheels, and tools you need.
- Check that both sides of the drivetrain can be built the same way.
- Decide where the battery and Control Hub will go before tightening everything.
- Make sure moving parts will not hit the frame, wires, or floor.
- Ask before cutting, drilling, forcing parts, or changing the team design.

## Space Planning

Leave room for:

- Control Hub
- battery
- power switch
- wires and connectors
- scoring mechanism
- intake or shooter, if your team uses one
- hands and tools for repairs

If a part fits only when nothing else is installed, it does not really fit yet.

## Safety Reminders

- Wear safety glasses when required.
- Keep fingers away from pinch points and spinning wheels.
- Do not test motors while someone is adjusting the drivetrain.
- Put tools down when carrying the robot.
- Keep batteries away from metal tools and loose hardware.
- Tell the teacher or mentor if a wire, battery, charger, or motor feels hot or looks damaged.
- Clean up loose fasteners so they do not end up inside the robot.
