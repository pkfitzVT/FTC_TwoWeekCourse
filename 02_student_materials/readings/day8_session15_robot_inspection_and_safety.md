# Session 15 Reading: Robot Inspection and Safety

## Inspection Is Part of Engineering

Inspection is not just a rule. It is part of engineering.

Teams inspect robots before competition for safety, fairness, and reliability. Race cars are inspected before motorsport events. Airplanes are inspected before they are allowed to fly. Spacecraft go through extensive checks before launch. Construction equipment and machinery are inspected before use.

Inspection protects your team, other teams, volunteers, judges, and the equipment.

## Inspection Vocabulary

**Inspection** is a careful check to make sure the robot is safe, follows the size rules, has secure wiring, and can operate without creating avoidable problems.

**Safety** means protecting people and equipment from harm.

**Reliability** means the robot can work repeatedly without constant repair.

A **requirement** is something the robot must do or follow.

A **constraint** is a limit your team must work within, such as size, time, materials, or safety.

A **checklist** is a list used to make sure important steps are not missed.

A **sharp edge** is an exposed edge that could cut someone or damage a game element.

A **pinch point** is a place where fingers, wires, or parts could get squeezed by moving pieces.

A **loose wire** is a wire that can move into wheels, gears, the flywheel, or other moving parts.

**Strain relief** means securing a wire so pulling on it does not pull directly on the connector.

The **power switch** is the switch used to turn robot power on and off.

A **battery mount** is the part or strap that keeps the battery from sliding around.

An **18-inch cube** is a size check used in FTC-style robots. The robot must fit inside the allowed starting size or course size constraint.

The **starting configuration** is how the robot is positioned before a match or demonstration begins.

**Field-ready** means the robot is safe, charged, connected, and ready to run on the field.

**Fail-safe** means a design helps prevent a dangerous or confusing failure.

**Compliance** means following the rules or requirements.

**Readiness** means the robot and team are prepared to demonstrate safely.

## Why Engineers Use Inspection Checklists

Checklists help teams catch problems before they become dangerous or frustrating.

Examples:

- A loose wire can get pulled into a wheel.
- A sharp edge can cut someone or damage a game element.
- A battery that moves around can pull on wires or change robot balance.
- A hidden power switch can make it hard to shut off the robot.
- A robot that does not fit the size limit may not be allowed to compete.

Good teams inspect because they expect small problems to appear. Finding them early is professional.

## Inspection Is Not About Blame

Finding a problem during inspection is useful. It means the team can fix it before the demonstration.

Inspection is not a test of whether your team is "good" or "bad." It is a process for finding problems early enough to fix them.

If your team finds a problem, record it, fix it, and retest it.
