# Session 16 Reading: Presentation Story and Script

## Tell the Robot Story Clearly

Your team needs to tell the story of your robot clearly and quickly. The presentation should be about 4 minutes, leaving 3 to 5 minutes for judge questions.

Short presentations are like elevator pitches. You need to get to the big ideas quickly.

Your presentation is not just a list of parts. It is a story about design, problems, choices, teamwork, and growth. Visuals, humor, and specific examples can help when they support the evidence.

## Presentation Vocabulary

A **presentation** is a short explanation given to an audience.

A **script** is a planned version of what speakers will say.

An **elevator pitch** is a short explanation that quickly communicates the most important ideas.

An **audience** is the group listening to the presentation.

A **judge** is a person who asks questions and looks for evidence of robot design, teamwork, and learning.

A **hook** is an opening that makes people want to listen.

**Evidence** is proof from sketches, photos, tests, code, or observations.

A **visual** is something people can see, such as the robot, a photo, a sketch, or a test table.

A **prototype** is an early version of a design.

A **design choice** is a decision about how the robot is built or programmed.

A **tradeoff** is what your team gave up when choosing one option over another.

An **iteration** is a changed version made after testing.

A **failure** is something that did not work yet and gave the team information.

An **improvement** is a change that made the robot or team work better.

**Teamwork** means how the team communicated, shared roles, solved conflict, and supported each other.

A **growth narrative** is a story about how the team changed and learned over time.

**Question-and-answer** is the part where judges ask follow-up questions.

A **time limit** is the maximum amount of time the presentation should take.

## Tell the Story of the Build

Include:

- what problem or challenge you were trying to solve,
- what your first idea was,
- what you built,
- what went wrong,
- what you changed,
- what worked better after revision,
- what each team member contributed,
- what you are proud of,
- and what you would improve next.

## A Simple 4-Minute Structure

| Time | Focus | Example |
|---|---|---|
| 0:00-0:30 | Hook and team introduction | "Our robot started as a simple chassis, but our biggest challenge became feeding one ball at a time." |
| 0:30-1:15 | Robot overview | Explain drivetrain, shooter, hopper, and trigger. |
| 1:15-2:15 | Engineering challenge | Describe one major problem and how the team fixed it. |
| 2:15-3:00 | Testing and evidence | Share what changed after testing. |
| 3:00-3:40 | Teamwork and growth | Explain how the team worked together and improved. |
| 3:40-4:00 | Closing | State what award or recognition the team believes it earned and why. |

## Use Visuals

Students can use:

- robot itself,
- photos of early prototypes,
- sketches,
- test tables,
- before/after examples,
- short demonstration,
- notebook pages,
- and simple diagrams.

## Humor Helps, But Evidence Matters

Humor can make the presentation memorable, but the team still needs evidence.

A funny story about a jam or failed test is stronger when students explain what they learned from it.
