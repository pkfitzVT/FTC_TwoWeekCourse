# Session 7 Reading: Mounting Motors and Wheels

## Why Mounting Matters

A drivetrain works only if the motor, wheel, axle, and frame all work together. A powerful motor will not help if it is loose. A good wheel will not help if it rubs against the frame or slips on the shaft.

Good mounting makes the robot easier to drive, easier to repair, and safer to test.

## Drivetrain Vocabulary

A **motor** changes electrical energy into spinning motion.

A **motor shaft** is the spinning metal part coming out of the motor.

An **axle** is a rod that supports a wheel. Some wheels spin on an axle, and some axles spin with the wheel.

A **coupler** connects one rotating part to another, such as a motor shaft to an axle.

A **hub** is the center connector that helps attach a wheel, gear, sprocket, or pulley to a shaft or axle.

A **wheel hub** is the part of the wheel that connects it to the shaft or axle.

A **set screw** is a small screw in a hub or coupler that tightens against a shaft. If it is loose, the shaft may spin without turning the wheel.

A **gear** is a toothed wheel that transfers motion to another gear.

A **sprocket** is a toothed wheel used with chain.

A **pulley** is a wheel used with a belt or string.

**Direct drive** means the wheel is powered directly by the motor shaft or by a very short connection.

A **driven wheel** is a wheel that receives power from a motor. A support wheel may touch the floor but not be powered.

**Traction** is grip between the wheel and the floor.

**Friction** is rubbing force. Some friction gives traction, but too much friction inside the robot can waste motor power.

**Alignment** means parts are straight and lined up correctly.

**Wobble** is unwanted shaking or uneven spinning.

**Torque** is twisting force. More torque helps a robot push or start moving.

**Speed** is how fast the wheel or robot moves.

## What Must Be Firm

The motor must be attached firmly to the frame. If the motor shifts, the wheel or shaft may move out of alignment.

The wheel must be attached so power from the motor actually turns the wheel. If a hub, coupler, or set screw is loose, the motor may spin while the robot stays still.

The left and right sides should be as similar as possible. If one side rubs or sits crooked, the robot may turn even when the code tells it to drive straight.

## Common Beginner Problems

- The wheel is loose.
- The set screw is not tightened.
- The motor is mounted crooked.
- The wheel rubs against the frame.
- Left and right wheels are not aligned.
- One side has more friction than the other.
- The robot frame flexes when the motor starts.
- Wires or zip ties touch a wheel.

## Test Before Driving

Before putting the robot on the floor, check:

- Can the wheel spin freely?
- Is the wheel secure?
- Is the motor secure?
- Does anything rub?
- Are both sides built the same way?
- Do wires stay away from wheels and shafts?
- Can your team reach the fasteners later if something loosens?

## What to Do If the Robot Turns When It Should Go Straight

First, do not assume the code is wrong. A robot can turn because of a mechanical issue.

Check whether both wheels are the same size and mounted at the same height. Spin each side by hand with the robot powered off. If one side feels harder to turn, look for rubbing, crooked mounting, or a tight axle.

Then check that both drive motors are secure and that both powered wheels actually receive power from the motors. A loose hub on one side can make the robot curve or spin.

After the mechanical check, your team can look at motor direction in the code and configuration.
